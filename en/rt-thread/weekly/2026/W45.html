I. Weekly Highlights

  • 31 merges in one day on 11-05, clearing a backlog spanning four months (11-05): The main repository merged 49 proposals this week, distributed by day as six on 11-02, four on 11-03, three on 11-04, thirty-one on 11-05, and five on 11-06. The 11-05 batch was highly concentrated in time—17 landed within the 31 minutes from 10:47 to 11:18, another 13 from 13:23 to 18:40, and the final one, an ARM Kconfig split, landed separately at 22:26. The batch composition shows this was not “written the same day, merged the same day”: many were opened in mid-to-late October, with the oldest, #11558, opened on July 5, and #11892 and #11897 both opened on 10-18. The merger structure is also clear: usualtreee with 7, BernardXiong with 5, cms19859230182-lang with 4, adilalperenciftci with 2, and the remaining 13 from various contributors—the division of labor where external contributors submit patches and maintainers validate and batch-merge has taken shape.

  • Version numbering and release process documented formally in the repository for the first time (11-04): Proposal #11955 was merged on 11-04 at 10:44, with 751 lines added across 14 files: bilingual (Chinese and English) “Version Numbering Rules and Implementation Details” and “Release Process,” accompanied by 7 flowcharts and registered in the Doxygen index. The rules put the cadence in numbers: Z revisions原则上 one per month, Y minor versions every 3 to 6 months, X major versions with no fixed cycle; Z must not introduce new public APIs, components, or driver frameworks, and a single BSP that brings in a new driver framework must be bumped to Y or X; after an official release, any issue found can only be addressed with X.Y.Z+1, and the original Tag, original Release content, or original deliverables must not be modified. Document examples are uniformly written as v6.0.0, v6.1.0, v6.1.1, and the next mainline is planned on the 6 series.

  • Attack surface shifts from syscall arguments to device firmware, with patches for both chains within hours (11-05 ~ 11-06): The MSI-X table flaw #11985 was reported on 11-05 at 01:12: a malicious PCIe device fills the BIR in a read-only configuration register with 6, aliasing out of bounds into pdev->rom, after which the mapping base becomes an absolute physical address of 0 plus offset, allowing arbitrary physical memory writes at boot time; #11986 posted a fix at 07:21 and was merged on 11-05 at 10:47, closing the loop within two days from report to merge. The next day, ivshmem’s #11992 was reported at 00:54: the device describes “shared memory” as a kernel memory region, and once the window is registered as /dev/ivshmemN, any unprivileged process can read and write kernel physical memory at byte granularity; #11993 was posted at 03:05, adding a default-off RT_IVSHMEM_JAILHOUSE option—two hours and eleven minutes from report to proposal. Both share the same root cause: a single missing check—rt_ioremap() performs no physical address validity check and maps whatever the device says.

  • The family-wide cleanup of RT-Smart syscall boundaries closed out this week, with only mailbox converted to a structural TODO (11-02 ~ 11-07): The graphics device negative offset #11969 got #11970 after 31 minutes; pipe’s #11958 was fixed by #11971 and merged; getaddrinfo’s #11977 was redone by a maintainer and merged; the GPT partition head-tail inversion #11978 was merged; timerfd and POSIX timer’s #11984 and #11980 received #11996 and #11997 on 11-07 at 03:04. The remaining mailbox #11960 received a maintainer reply on 11-07 at 03:05: confirming that sys_mb_send() / sys_mb_send_wait() forward user object pointers directly to rt_mb_*, and that RT_ASSERT only reads the type field in the object and cannot substitute for an ownership check; noting that this requires a structural fix via LWP object AVL lookup plus reference holding, and that the same check should also cover semaphore, mutex, event, message queue, and timer paths; and that this round did not submit an incomplete fix due to the absence of an RT-Smart cross toolchain.

  • Wuzhen “New Light”: the AIOS foundation enters a national-level platform’s product recommendation list (11-02 ~ 11-03): The 2026 World Internet Conference Wuzhen Summit was held November 2–5, with 671 Chinese and foreign enterprises and institutions from 54 countries and regions exhibiting, and 35 “New Light” products and 20 “New Shine” scenarios selected and recommended. RT-Thread’s entry was the “RT-Thread Heterogeneous Converged Edge-Side Intelligent AIOS Foundation,” and Ecosystem Director Li Yan gave a keynote on-site at the expo titled “Virtualized Hybrid Deployment Embodied Intelligence Solution · AI Kit.” The technical proposition is stated clearly: AI inference on the Linux side, real-time control on the RT-Thread side, the two sides running independently, so that if the AI side fails the control side can still execute—officially claimed control precision down to the microsecond level; the accompanying AI Kit bundles the inference engine, model optimization, hardware adaptation, and debugging environment into one toolchain. The significance lies in the narrative framing—previously pitched externally as a “real-time control foundation,” this time the selected vehicle is an edge-side intelligent operating system, with the landing point being robot “brain” and “cerebellum” coordination.

Software Packages and Middleware

Date: 2026-11-04 ~ 2026-11-06　Source: PR env #304 (serverless index for plugin marketplace)

  • Env plugin marketplace switched to a serverless index (merged 11-06 07:05): The sdk repo adds an env-plugins directory, with one subdirectory per plugin, each holding an index.json; the env repo implements reading this index and downloading .epack installers from GitHub Releases. The change is +1108/-48 across 18 files on the env side (marketplace logic, WebUI frontend, CLI subcommands, and five test groups), and +125 lines across four files on the sdk side. The externally visible change is that the index is read from the local SDK repo by default, with no need to deploy a marketplace server; explicitly setting an environment variable or running rt-env plugin market set still prioritizes the HTTP marketplace, and rt-env plugin market clear reverts. The first listed plugin is the RT-Kconfig configurator 0.1.34, whose download record points to a real asset in that repo’s Release.
  • The security boundary is finer than expected: Download size is capped, returning 413 when exceeded; redirects must be same-host and same-protocol; index paths cannot escape the env-plugins directory; plugin IDs are regex-constrained and duplicates are detected. The cost is that the trust boundary shifts from “official marketplace” to “repo maintainer”—third-party plugins no longer wait for review; publishing a Release and adding an index entry is enough to distribute.
  • coact (pushed 11-04 22:35): A C++17 active-object and hierarchical state machine framework, MIT-licensed, 98 stars. Events replace threads, behavior is written as hierarchical state machines, transition tables are constexpr, and there are no runtime allocations; the same headers run on RT-Thread and FreeRTOS, and also compile on Linux and Windows via a PAL. Three properties are enforced as structural constraints: bounded (queues, memory pools, and batch sizes are all compile-time constants, with no heap and no new／delete), detectable (SubmitResult return codes, Monitor counters, Breaker::level()), and recoverable (each active object has a five-state circuit breaker). Its value to RT-Thread lies in application-layer methodology—most projects in the ecosystem pile up logic directly with threads and message queues, lacking a framework that constrains boundedness, observability, and recoverability together.
  • ulog observability enhancement (pending review): #11987 makes ulog output the calling function, file, and line number, +156 lines across four files, with updates still coming as of 11-06 21:05.

V. Industry Applications and Business Trends

Industrial Control and Industrial Connectivity

Date: 2026-11-02 ~ 2026-11-03　Source: Repo: rtthread-industrial-gateway

  • An industrial gateway with the entire chain on-board: A newly created personal project implements all four links of an industrial data gateway on a single STM32F407VGT6 — downstream, FreeModbus acts as an RTU master, periodically polling the holding registers of field slaves; locally, LVGL 8.3 provides the HMI, with button control and readback sharing a single holding-register mirror table; upstream, an ML307 4G module carries MQTT QoS1 to ThingsBoard, with RPC-based reverse control; for remote upgrades, HTTP chunked download plus CRC32 verification writes to the download partition, after which rtboot moves the image to the app partition and jumps to it. The hardware configuration is documented in more detail than most comparable repos: an FSMC 8080 parallel 16-bit TFT, an xpt2046 resistive touchscreen over SPI, a W25Q128 (16 MB) on SPI1 with chip select software-controlled via PG15, the ML307 on uart6 using AT commands, and the Modbus slave on uart3 at 9600 8N1.
  • Dual-MCU disinfection device: “Jingxun Weishi” targets small and medium public areas such as classrooms, corridors, and public seating. The architecture uses a GD32H759IMT6 (Cortex-M7) as the motion main controller and an STM32F103C8T6 (Cortex-M3) for spray control, both running RT-Thread 4.1.1. Features cover four-wheel differential movement, robotic-arm atomized spraying, HX711 load-cell weighing for remaining-liquid monitoring, remote control, scheduled disinfection, status reporting, and video backhaul. The repo structure is tidier than most competition projects: docs/ contains hardware notes, the communication protocol, code style, and submission documents; scripts/ contains Keil build and repo self-check scripts; and a clang-format config is included. The trade-off is clear: motion control demands real-time responsiveness, while spraying and remaining-liquid monitoring are slow variables; splitting them leaves headroom on each chip, and a single serial protocol suffices for the communication cost.

Motor Control and Edge AI

Date: 2026-10-30 ~ 2026-11-04　Source: Repo: NS800RT7P65D FOC and CANopen

  • 20 kHz sensorless FOC on dual Cortex-M7: The NOVOSENSE NS800RT7P65D is split into two logical CPUs — one runs 20 kHz ADC sampling and PWM, performing sensorless FOC, sliding-mode observer, and PLL estimation with hardware shutdown; the other runs UART and FinSH, CANopenNode v4.1 with CiA 402 bridging, and CAN transmit/receive. Cross-core communication uses only an asynchronous command mailbox and a consistent six-field, 24-byte snapshot, avoiding haphazard shared-memory reads; the foundation is RT-Thread 5.3.1 in an SMP configuration. This is the competition entry closest to production form this week.
  • On-device MLP for vibration diagnostics: EdgeDoctor-RT on the Infineon PSoC Edge E84 processes a vibration data block every 20 ms, extracts 64-dimensional band features over a 1-second sliding window, and feeds them to a 64-32-5 MLP that classifies five states — normal, bearing inner-race fault, bearing outer-race fault, misalignment, and imbalance — along with confidence and recommended action. It is based on the official Edgi_Talk_M55_XiaoZhi v1.4.0, layering only application code without modifying boot, linking, HAL, or Wi-Fi resources.
  • Two kinds of push-down in motion control and inspection: On the NXP FRDM-MCXA346, one project does closed-loop stepper positioning (encoder feedback plus a Python and Tkinter host application, running 10-degree and 90-degree positioning, a 45-degree relative reversal, and return to software zero), while another builds an inspection cart with ultrasonic obstacle avoidance and smoke detection, with vision recognition kept on the PC side. Both examples illustrate how far competition boards have been pushed down: the same general-purpose MCU carries both closed-loop motion control and low-speed inspection.
  • The shared trade-off across four vision projects: Models are compressed onto edge NPUs or MCUs, complex judgments go to the cloud or a rules engine, and raw imagery never leaves the device — the same engineering judgment as the Ruiqing desktop pet, with “the brain on the PC and the body on the board.”

Wearables and Verification Platforms

Date: 2026-11-02 ~ 2026-11-03　Source: Repo: helios-sf32 (Sifli SF32LB52 watch firmware)

  • Smartwatch firmware on the Sifli SF32LB52: Based on the OpenSifli SDK, targeting the LCD screen of the SF32LB52 development board, with an LVGL UI on an RT-Thread base; the repo was created on September 4 and saw one push within the window. Wearables are a relatively rare landing spot for RT-Thread, and the constraints of such products are the opposite of industrial boards — smooth screen refresh, low standby power, and hot-updatable UI resources — and the OpenSifli SDK adopting RT-Thread as its default RTOS shows that this product line chose its base at the SDK level.
  • An RV32IM dual-issue FPGA CPU uses Nano as its minimal verification stack: A pipelined CPU project targeting Xilinx Kintex-7, with the RV32IM instruction set, machine-mode CSRs, and timer interrupts; the main execution channel plus an integer side channel supports dual-instruction pairing; the front end has a BTB, RAS, and indirect-jump prediction; and the memory side has dual-channel forwarding, load/store pairing, and cache-hit bypass. On the software side it runs RT-Thread Nano v3.1.5 with a UART command line and CoreMark 1.0. The verification data is more detailed than typical hobby projects: with timingopt15 plus a 256-line DCache and an ideal 180 MHz clock, a 100-iteration behavioral simulation of CoreMark yields 31,445,017 CPU cycles, 30,605,777 retired instructions, CPI 1.027421, a final CRC of 0x988c, and 7 directed regressions passing; the shortcomings are also stated — after 180 MHz routing, WNS is -1.371 ns and WHS is +0.013 ns, so timing has not converged and no bitstream has been generated yet. For RT-Thread, Nano once again serves as the minimal runnable software stack for CPU verification, exposing problems in interrupt, context-switch, and timer paths better than bare-metal tests.

VI. Trend Observations

  • The backlog clearance peaked this week and was a one-off: 31 of the 49 merges landed on 11-05, spanning four months (the oldest, #11558, was opened on 7-05, and several were opened between 10-18 and 10-29). The direct implication for downstream is that pulling master over these two days will surface a large batch of changes, concentrating regression-testing cost rather than spreading it across days. The stable metric for judging project health remains queue turnaround time—28 new items were opened this week and 49 merged, so inflow and outflow were roughly balanced and the queue did not get thinner.
  • The attack surface has shifted once: from syscall arguments to device firmware. Reports from late October to early November concerned missing validation of “value to object” (nested pointers in getaddrinfo, the ioctl pointer in pipe, the fd type in timerfd, the id in POSIX timer, the raw pointer in block device partitions); this week’s two switched to “believe whatever the device says”—a single out-of-bounds bit in MSI-X’s BIR aliases to the rom member, and ivshmem’s config word directly describes kernel memory as shared memory. The common technical gap is that rt_ioremap() does not validate physical addresses; the recommendation is to extract this into a unified layer rather than checking it separately in each driver.
  • “Report to patch” has reached the hour level, and the bottleneck has moved to review and verification: this week’s three samples were 31 minutes (#11969 → #11970), six hours and nine minutes (#11985 → #11986), and two hours and eleven minutes (#11992 → #11993). Reporters provided complete material with PoC, GDB logs, and version tracing, so maintainers could patch directly upon receipt—this compresses time more than any process rule. The cost is that review pressure is pushed downstream: 48 items await review, the block device partition item has been pending for more than three days, and the mailbox item explicitly states that it “needs a structural fix and no incomplete patch will be submitted this round.”
  • Version governance has moved from documentation to evidence, and mainline planning already looks toward the 6 series: the three rules—Z monthly, Y every three to six months, X irregular—have been written into the repository documentation, and the accompanying #11972 breaks “can a Z release ship” into per-framework QEMU and utest checklists; within a week, three framework test suites were merged and two were hooked into CI. Documentation examples are uniformly written as v6.0.0 / v6.1.0 / v6.1.1, indicating that the next mainline round is planned around the 6 series—for downstream distributions, this means there may be no 5.5 after 5.4, and migration-window judgments must be recalculated on that premise.
  • The external narrative has steadily shifted to “edge intelligent operating system,” backed by engineering samples: at Wuzhen, “Xinguang” put the AIOS foundation on a national-level platform’s recommendation list, and AI Kit discussed virtualized hybrid deployment; in the same week, the Ruiqing desktop-pet retrospective gave a reproducible seven-step chain of “board-side real-time IO plus PC-side inference,” the open-source AIOS alliance draft gave three types of Agent solutions and the division of labor among three partners, and the four vision projects among the competition entries consistently compressed models to the edge while sending complex judgments to the cloud. For the first time, the promotional messaging and the engineering trade-offs match.
  • The board-level ecosystem is moving in two directions, and kernel pluggability has been validated by external projects: on one side, vendors are scripting BSP repositories and releasing them in batches at the same version (HPMicro’s 12 repos released the same version on the same day) to ensure consistency across boards; on the other, the community is filling in BSPs for third-party boards (Wildfire HPM6750 BTB) and older platforms (Nuvoton N9H30) to cover the long tail. At the same time, the community replaced the kernel with RT-Thread as an ESP-IDF component on the ESP32-S31, which only provides FreeRTOS, and passed 36 self-checks; porting proposals for new architectures such as RH850 have also entered the queue—”kernel pluggability” has gone from a slogan to a reproducible engineering result.
