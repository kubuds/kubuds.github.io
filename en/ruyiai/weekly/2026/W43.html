RuyiAI Weekly Report (2026-10-19 ~ 2026-10-23)

2026-10-23 Kubuds

Reporting period: Monday, October 19, 2026 through Friday, October 23, 2026 — 5 working days.

Sources: 5 issues of this publication’s daily RuyiAI update reports (2026-10-19 to 2026-10-23; each issue covers the window from 04:30 the previous day to 04:30 the current day, with Monday covering the past 3 days including the weekend); plus a 168-hour repository audit of three organizations — RuyiAI-Stack, ruyisdk, and buddy-compiler — a supplementary audit of key repositories in openRuyi-Project, dual-source verification against the RuyiSDK developer community and the openRuyi distribution news page, and three-page snapshots of the official RuyiAI homepage (audit coverage extends to the morning of 10-24).

Editorial note: Three main threads ran in parallel this week. First, the main openRuyi repository completed an overall pivot to “packaging by dependency” across 637 commits, progressing from filling in the Rust crate closure, to adding Python cloud storage backends, to removing vendoring from Go, and on to completing source checksums and %autochangelog specification compliance. Second, whole-branch replays of the two riscv branches — PyTorch and LLVM — converged from “scheduled as needed” to “daily”; the set of patch titles is unchanged, while commit hashes expire daily. Third, the compiler, simulator, and operator layers of the RISC-V matrix extensions (AME and Ztt) all landed this week simultaneously, forming a minimal closed loop that “compiles, executes, and produces correct results.” Beyond the daily-report material, the weekly report adds seven newly discovered audit findings, all falling on the daytime of 10-23 (after the daily-report window) or at period boundaries, listed in the appendix.

  • openRuyi lands 637 commits in five days, packaging strategy shifts wholesale to “package by dependency”: By calendar day, 10-19 through 10-23 saw 221, 137, 51, 109, and 119 commits respectively, dominated by Leetfs (333) and Jingwiw (152). The progression is clearly traceable—10-19 alone had 211 Rust-related commits (roughly 200 new packages) and advanced the network storage stack (dpdk 26.07, spdk 26.09, liburing 2.15, btrfs-progs 7.1, nvme-cli 3.1); 10-20 filled out the full fsspec cloud storage backend family with a symmetric structure of 42 commits each for Rust/Go/Python; 10-21 through 10-22 landed the de-vendoring rework of minio (merged 10-20), sonobuoy, and umoci (merged 10-21) on main, and added the import paths required after vendor removal, while a similar proposal for picoclaw is still queued; from 10-22 the work shifted to spec completion, with Jingwiw sweeping 54 packages in one pass to add source checksums and switch to %autochangelog.   • Whole-branch replays of two riscv branches settle into daily operations: PyTorch replays five mornings running, LLVM breaks eight days of silence with three consecutive rebases: The PyTorch riscv branch was rewritten five days straight from 10-18 to 10-22, with heads at 8b254cfd, 44826095, 7f49d33f, 3c1ea315, and 29259d90, each time uniformly rewriting committer timestamps across the branch; the LLVM riscv branch, silent for eight days since 10-13, rebased three times in a row on 10-21, 10-22, and 10-23, with heads at dd05e5b5, 58d62a47, and 7c77126b.   • Ztt matrix extension lands for the first time on the AME branch of ruyisdk/llvm-project (10-23 17:15), completing the matrix extension chain together with the AME trio: On the last workday of the week, the AME branch landed “[RISCV] Add initial Ztt matrix extension support based on spec v0.6”, its second commit after the AME CI workflow merged on 10-19. Together with the AME patch series on the RuyiAI-side LLVM branch (extension and backend path design #16, #24, #27; instruction updates #29; IME builtin lowering and A100 HP/SP scheduling #30, #31, #32), QEMU’s ame-0.6 branch, and the AME-operator operator library, the matrix extension is now complete across all three layers: compilation, simulation, and operators.   • QEMU completes full instruction simulation for P extension v0.6, with GCC and LLVM aligned in step: The p-dev branch of ruyisdk/qemu (based on upstream v11.1.0) landed a 12-commit wrap-up batch at 10-21 16:35, all focused on packed SIMD—Q-format multiply and multiply-add, pure multiply, multiply-accumulate, two-way and four-way multiply-add/subtract, horizontal reduction, load-copy, pack/merge/leading count, RV32-only instructions—plus removal of P’s dependency on Zbkb to align with the new spec and additions to tests/tcg/riscv. Paper: restructuring FlashAttention on long-vector processors yields 1.84x to 2.08x measured speedup

arXiv 2610.29489v1, “Reorienting the Vector Axis: Restructuring FlashAttention for Long-Vector Processors” (Emmanuel Irabor, Marc Casas), ports PyTorch’s CPU FlashAttention kernel to a RISC-V CPU with 2KB vector registers, starting with a blunt baseline: only 16.2% of peak floating-point throughput.

The cause analysis is specific: the original kernel advances row by row over query rows, vectorizing the multiply with V along the 64-element head dimension; softmax’s per-row maximum and sum are reductions that “collapse an entire register into a scalar”, and the polynomial coefficients of the software exponential function also compete with tile data for vector registers, causing spills to the stack—the three together keep vector register utilization low. Neither fix changes the algorithm or numerics: first, transpose the score tile together with the running output so queries fall on a linearly contiguous axis, so that the single parameter “how many queries fit in a tile” uniformly determines the vector length of every kernel section, and the two reductions naturally disappear; second, move the polynomial coefficients into scalar registers, eliminating spills and cutting softmax’s vector memory instructions by 78.7%.

Measured: at head dimension 64, the two together bring whole-kernel cycles down to a 1.84x to 2.08x speedup, raising floating-point utilization from 16.2% to 29.7%; an FPGA of the same design ranks the two changes consistently and gains more, reaching 2.71x. The significance for this stack is methodological rather than numerical: vector-length adaptation and register pressure are precisely the structural problems triton-riscv and tilelang-riscv face in RVV lowering, not “whether intrinsics exist”; the paper’s “match queries per tile to the machine” and IREE’s setting Tile N to vlen/8 are the same class of decision and can be compared directly on equal terms. Source: arXiv 2610.29489

Kernel and boot-path intelligence: read-only mapping proposal, C907/E907 boot state, K3 PCIe patch v7

VII. Trend Watch

Packaging unit shifts from “source tree” to “dependency package”; this week completes the pivot from closing the closure to completing the spec

This week’s 637 commits have a single throughline: changing “vendor with the source” to “package by dependency.” On the Rust side, the crate closure was completed (including the 23 rust-uv-* packages for the uv build backend); on the Python side, all mainstream cloud backends for fsspec were filled in; on the Go side, vendor was removed and the import paths required after the deleted directories were restored one by one. The order itself shows that the completion condition for de-vendoring is not deleting the vendor directory, but ensuring every depended-upon module can be resolved in the distribution under its original import path—without the import-path restoration, deleting vendor would simply cause immediate compile failures.

By 10-22 the focus had shifted from “can it build” to “can it be audited”: 54 commits added source checksums and switched to %autochangelog, covering a range of package names from m to x. Source checksums make build inputs verifiable; %autochangelog makes changelogs generated from commit history rather than hand-written. Once both are in place, CVE fixes propagate along a single unified path. The indirect value to RuyiAI lies in upper-layer usability: PyTorch’s DataLoader, checkpoint, and dataset tooling generally access remote storage via fsspec, so whether the backends are complete directly determines whether training scripts can run on K1/K3—this portion of dependencies that the distribution fills in for the upper layer matters more to “can it be used” than any single operator optimization.

The release channel becomes the new bottleneck: both the package manager and the index go silent, and the cause is not code

Two phenomena coexist this week: the ruyi package manager stays unchanged at 0.53.0 for the entire month, with 0.54.0 slated for late October; packages-index has had zero commits since 10-13. Issue 079’s explanation was an ISCAS software-source data synchronization anomaly, where already-built packages failed to refresh to the public network; combined with the team’s investment in the summit, this creates the contrast of “hundreds of commits per day on the code side, no new packages visible on the user side.”

This suggests that evaluating distribution progress cannot rely on commit counts alone. Filling in packages, checksums, and dependency paths all prepare for final delivery, but if any link is stuck in the release channel, the user’s actual experience is stagnation. The next observation point is whether 0.54.0 ships its alpha on schedule at month’s end, and whether the ISCAS synchronization issue is resolved alongside the 2026.10 release.

Replay moves from “scheduled” to “daily”: the two riscv branches converge on the same operations model, but review remains the real metronome

PyTorch performed a whole-branch rewrite on five consecutive mornings from 10-18 to 10-22, while LLVM, after eight days of silence, rebased three times in a row on 10-21, 10-22, and 10-23. The common features are that committer timestamps are uniformly rewritten, the set of patch titles is unchanged, and commit hashes expire daily; the only difference is span—LLVM led by 1769 commits on the first day (eight days of backlog), then about 275 per day for the next two (routine single-day catch-up).

The engineering significance is decoupling “following upstream” from “maintaining local patches,” at the cost of traceable history: any downstream that references by hash (image builds, CI caches, issue reproduction) breaks that same day or after each rebase, so these two branches should be versioned by the set of patch titles rather than by hash. More noteworthy is that no new patches were added this week—PyTorch’s seven open PRs and LLVM’s two FPGA PRs only refreshed timestamps, with no new review comments and no merges. The replay mechanism is now solid; the real metronome has become the digestion rate of the review queue. PyTorch paused replay for the first time on 10-23; whether this is a pace adjustment needs confirmation in the next cycle.

The extension landing path takes shape: compile, simulate, and operator layers must all be present to count as usable; once complete, the bottleneck shifts to hardware testing

Taking AME as an example, this week shows a complete path from proposal to usability for an extension: first the backend design and instruction-set definitions (LLVM #16, #24, #27, #29), then the lowering and scheduling implementation (#30, #31, #32), alongside the simulator providing executable semantics (QEMU ame-0.6), and finally the operator library providing testable kernels and a verification suite (AME-operator). Ztt also filled in one side this week—the AME branch of ruyisdk/llvm-project landed initial support for spec v0.6, corresponding to the GCC-side Ztt Md support and QEMU’s Ztt instruction-family implementation.

The P extension follows the same path but is already in its latter half: QEMU covers all instructions (a 12-commit finishing batch), GCC is updated to 0.6, and on the LLVM side intrinsics such as mulq, multiply-high accumulate, sati/usati, and Packed Pair are merged upstream in batches. By comparison, the P extension lacks application-side benchmarks, and AME lacks real hardware. This order is worth recording as a method: without a simulator you cannot verify the compiler, and without an operator library you cannot verify the simulator; before all three are present, talking about performance is meaningless, and only after they are complete does the bottleneck shift to hardware and real measurements. This week’s progress is “completeness,” not “faster.”

The RVV backend’s focus shifts from “are the instructions there” to “tiling and threading”

IREE walked the precision ladder from f32 to f16 in two days, reusing the same lowerRiscvVFmaccLike and the same accumulator legalization both times; the order is to first guarantee a correct path (f32), then pick the fastest one according to extension availability (native f16, widened f16, +zvfhmin fallback), with the cost model pricing the three paths separately. Measurements on X60 make the bottleneck location clear: single-threaded inner_tiled is already faster than the mmt4d ukernel, while 4-thread and 8-thread remain slower.

The arXiv paper on long-vector FlashAttention restructuring reaches a conclusion from the same source: by transposing the score tile and the running output so that queries fall on the linear contiguous axis, the two reductions naturally disappear and register spills are eliminated, bringing whole-core cycles down to 1.84–2.08x and raising floating-point utilization from 16.2% to 29.7%. Both are saying the same thing—once single-core instruction selection is in place, what remains is tiling parameters and threading. For tilelang-riscv and triton-riscv, this is a roadmap that can be compared item by item: the choice of Tile N = vlen/8, the way the cost model is tiered, and the binding relationship between queries per tile and vector length are all worth comparing under the same terms in one’s own lowering, rather than only looking at “whether f16 is supported.”

Distributions begin laying out desktop and virtualization validation stacks; platform forms diverge into server and embedded paths

A group of aligned proposals has appeared in openRuyi’s open PR queue: enabling GUI test features in qemu (#1421), adding virglrenderer (#1415), fwupd support in ovmf (#1436), adding ydotool (#1457), and fixing a man-page conflict in xorriso (#1489). The five items span virtualization, graphics virtualization, firmware updates, input automation, and optical image tooling; together they point to “running a full desktop on RISC-V and automating its validation”—the other end of the same requirement as the “AI-driven IDE graphical testing” shown at the summit.

Platform forms are diverging at the same time. At the server level, represented by SpacemiT V100, UEFI, ACPI, and SMBIOS in the spec become hard requirements, and BRS compliance testing and the installation chain are the entry ticket; at the embedded and consumer level, represented by K1/K3, the path is board images, toolchains, and a support matrix.
