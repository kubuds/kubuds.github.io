I. Weekly Highlights

StarFive JH-B100 Enters Mass Production and Delivery; Dubhe-70 Brings a Domestic Out-of-Order Core into the Data Center (11-17, 11-19)

On November 17, StarFive announced that its server BMC chip, the Dubhe-B100 (JH-B100), has entered mass production and shipped to customers. The company says the chip has passed product introduction and review at multiple server vendors and internet companies, and that systems carrying it will soon enter mass production. Shipment volumes, customer names, and production scheduling were not disclosed.

On November 19, StarFive filled in the core model. Its in-house CPU IP, Dubhe-70, is what the chip carries. The company calls this the first time a domestic out-of-order RISC-V CPU IP has been commercially mass-produced at data-center scale, with annual shipments “expected” to reach the tens of millions.

Specifications per StarFive’s website and the two press releases. The JH-B100 is a quad-core in-house RISC-V design at 19,200 DMIPS, paired with the Dubhe-Star-500 coherent NoC, compatible with DDR4-3200 and DDR5-4800. It integrates PFR platform firmware resilience and PROT, and supports China’s SM2/SM3/SM4 cryptographic standards, secure boot, and DICE. Interfaces include dual PCIe 4.0 endpoints, a full-featured eSPI Slave, PECI, I2C/I3C, USB 3.2 Gen1, SGPIO, CAN-FD, IPMB, MCTP, and NCSI. The chip has two built-in LTPI channels to support dual-node management. The SDK follows Yocto and can connect directly to OpenBMC. Dubhe-70 has a 9-stage or deeper pipeline, three-issue superscalar, deep out-of-order execution, supports multi-core and I/O cache coherency, and implements the RV64GCBH instruction set.

The weight of this news lies in the position, not the performance. A BMC does not participate in computing, but every server needs one, and the slot has long been held by Arm and 8051-class controllers. Previously, RISC-V had only sampling and validation at this position. Choosing it as a beachhead follows the same logic as automotive chips entering through body control: performance requirements are far below those of a main CPU, the software stack is backed by OpenBMC and Yocto, and the validation cost of customer introduction is manageable. Once you get in, it is a long-term supply relationship.

There are two ecosystem endorsements. Intel completed validation of Intel CPU management functions on its CRB platform. AMI integrated the JH-B100 SDK into its OneTree BMC firmware product. Both were disclosed earlier at Intel Connection and the Dishui Lake Forum; this week they landed as “mass production and delivery” for the first time.

Three things need discounting. First, “soon to enter mass production” is the customer-side line; volume depends on system vendors’ production schedules, and BMC procurement decisions tend to follow the main CPU platform. Second, annual shipments in the tens of millions are an expectation, not an order book or delivered volume. Global server shipments are themselves on the order of tens of millions of units a year, so reaching ten million chips would mean capturing most of the incremental market. Third, Dubhe-70’s performance claims are inconsistent over time. At its August 2024 launch, the figure was 7.2 SPECint2006 per GHz, benchmarked against Cortex-A72/A510, with claims of 80% higher performance, 32% better energy efficiency, and 90% better area efficiency versus A55. This week’s mass-production release changes this to “close to Cortex-A73.” This newsletter records both side by side without adjudicating.

The industry-chain position also needs stating clearly. Domestic RISC-V mass-production cases in the data center are currently concentrated in secondary-core scenarios such as BMCs, edge gateways, and AI inference cards. There is still no public case of dual- or quad-socket main CPUs replacing x86 at volume. What this proves is that an out-of-order core can pass server-grade validation flows, not that general-purpose compute has been replaced. Two things to watch: whether JH-B100’s quarterly shipments materialize, and whether Dubhe-83/100 gets customer tape-outs.

Purism Swaps Its Flagship Linux Phone’s SoC for SpacemiT K1 (11-14, 11-17)

On November 14, Purism launched crowdfunding for the Librem 5 v2. This is the first time the series has moved its main SoC from Arm to RISC-V. The official page lists an eight-core SpacemiT K1, a 5.7-inch 720×1440 display, 8GB of RAM, and 128GB of storage. The battery is a 4500mAh replaceable unit with dual SIM support. There are also three hardware kill switches for cellular, Wi-Fi/Bluetooth, and microphone/camera. The OS is Debian-based PureOS, able to run Android apps through the Waydroid compatibility layer.

Pricing and timeline were disclosed at the same time. The crowdfunding price is $1,599, with a goal of 2,500 orders by February 15, 2027. Test units are expected in late 2027, with mass-production delivery pointing to around May 2028. A November 17 official press release refined the delivery line to “14 to 15 months after the campaign ends” and for the first time put “ARM64 application compatibility” on the must-solve list. CTO Jonathon Hall’s statement: “RISC-V is the future, and the Librem 5 v2 is the product that brings it to mainstream users.”

The risks are written on the official page. RISC-V is not yet a plug-and-boot platform for PureOS; the graphics stack can currently only display images, and full 3D support and optimization still need to be rewritten. The bottleneck is not the K1’s compute but the mobile graphics stack, camera pipeline, and power management—the parts that are “ready-made in the Arm ecosystem and must be redone on RISC-V.”

Placed alongside the week’s other news, the thread becomes clearer. Within two weeks, the K1 has entered both a Start9 router and a Purism phone—a rare case of a domestic RISC-V SoC running two device lines in parallel. In the same period, FIORA5 points to a 2027 prototype and 2029 licensing, while Lingrui Zhixin points to 2027 tape-out and 2028 mass production. The delivery windows for high-value devices and high-performance chips generally fall in 2027–2029. The two years in between are the window for software stacks and post-silicon validation.

Infineon Moves Its Flagship AURIX Family to RISC-V; riscv.org Publishes a Five-Question Interview (11-19)

On November 19, the RISC-V International blog published a five-question interview with Thomas Schneid, Infineon’s Vice President of Automotive Microcontroller Software and Ecosystem Management. The subject was the AURIX RC1, released on November 4, Infineon’s first microcontroller family based on the RISC-V instruction set.

The background figures in the interview are worth noting. According to TechInsights, Infineon supplied more than one-third of the world’s automotive microcontrollers in 2025. AURIX is the family the company’s automotive business was built on and the main support for its market position. The RC1 merges the existing TriCore and TRAVEO T2G product lines into one generation, covering everything from real-time controllers to MCU/MPU hybrid devices. Applications include zonal, body, chassis, battery management, powertrain, ADAS safety management, and cockpit. Some models will carry a parallel processing unit for AI acceleration.

Infineon emphasizes migration cost. Schneid’s line is that the company put substantial effort into minimizing both the engineering investment for automakers moving to RISC-V and the impact on customer toolchains. The weight of that statement: the switching cost for automotive MCUs lies mainly not in the chip itself but in AUTOSAR configuration, debuggers, functional safety certification, and supply-chain re-validation. A supplier with one-third of the market willing to move its flagship family suggests it judges the ecosystem sufficient.

There is also plenty of room to hedge. Infineon explicitly says specific models and product-line details have not been announced, meaning no SKUs, no sample timeline, and no mass-production node. Migration of existing AURIX customers will not happen overnight either; automotive-grade projects typically have three-to-five-year design cycles. Seeing the RC1 in a production vehicle will take until around 2029.

Read alongside StarFive’s BMC mass production, the 2026 industry signal for RISC-V is concentrated in the management and control planes, not yet in main compute. This is one of the heaviest architectural signals of the year, but what is missing right now is deliverable models and timelines.

RISE: Python riscv64 Native Wheels Pass 1,500, 519 Still Missing (11-18)

On November 18, the RISE project published a progress report, “Python on RISC-V: 1500 Packages and Counting,” by Ludovic Henry of Qualcomm. The report’s scope: the wheel dashboard tracks the 15,000 most-downloaded packages on PyPI, of which 2,076 contain native code and must be built separately for riscv64. As of November 18, 1,506 have riscv64 wheels.

The numbers need unpacking. Of the 1,506, 117 are published on PyPI by the upstream projects themselves, and 1,389 are provided by RISE’s own build index. Broken down by download rank: of the top 100, 92 are available and 5 missing; of the top 500, 438 available and 50 missing; of the top 1,000, 842 available and 133 missing; of all 2,076, 519 are still missing, about a quarter. In other words, for most users to install successfully, they must add a command-line flag pointing to the RISE index.

Packages already on the list include NumPy, SciPy, pandas, cryptography, PyArrow, PyTorch, and vLLM. PyTorch’s 2.13.0 wheels cover CPython 3.12, 3.13, 3.14, and the free-threaded 3.14 build, built on real RISC-V hardware rather than QEMU or cross-compilation, and passed 212,038 test cases. vLLM’s 0.29.0+cpu was merged on September 21, with 0.30.0 following on September 24; the first release produced CPython 3.12 ABI3 manylinux_2_39_riscv64 wheels.

The most notable item in the report is a comparison. On a VisionFive 2, building NumPy from source takes about 15 minutes, while installing the prebuilt wheel takes about 25 seconds. When a project’s dependency tree contains a dozen or so packages with extensions, that gap runs into hours. The report also describes an exit mechanism: once upstream projects start publishing riscv64 wheels themselves, the automation proposes retiring RISE’s build. The real metric for this progress is not how large the index is, but how many entries can be deleted because upstream has taken over.

Two caveats. First, this is a separate supply chain: 1,389 packages depend on RISE’s build and release process, and while there are immutable releases and SHA-256 digests, responsibility is separate from each package’s upstream maintainers. Second, the target platform is glibc Linux and does not cover MCU-class RISC-V running MicroPython or bare metal. Being installable does not mean running fast; vector optimization still has to be done separately.

Lingrui Zhixin Completes Several Hundred Million Yuan Pre-A+; P100 Targets a 128-Core Server Chip (11-13)

Shanghai Lingrui Zhixin announced on November 13 that it had completed a Pre-A+ round of several hundred million yuan. It was led by Meituan’s strategic investment arm, with participation from a JD.com-affiliated fund, Ganbei Fund, Lihe Suanxin Fund, Junxi Capital, SenseTime Guoxiang Capital, Shangqi Capital, and Amber Capital, and existing shareholders increasing their stakes. The company was founded in early 2025 and is registered in Zhangjiang, Shanghai, with cumulative funding approaching one billion yuan.

The core of the product side is the P100, a server-grade RISC-V CPU core released in January this year. A single core supports dynamic simultaneous multithreading with SMT4, switchable between SMT1 and SMT4 without rebooting the system, paired with a 20-stage-plus out-of-order pipeline and 8-decode, 10-issue. In August this year, a test chip based on the P100 was taped out on a domestic advanced process line, and in September the company disclosed it had entered the commercial stage. A 128-core server-grade CPU is planned for tape-out in 2027 and mass production in 2028.

The technical narrative is clear. Agent workloads are characterized by large numbers of concurrent sessions and frequent I/O waits; single-core throughput is less effective than converting wait time into another thread, and SMT4 is designed for exactly that. In the near term it sells IP licensing and custom development; only in the long term will it sell chips. That also explains why it can raise funding repeatedly, though customer names, orders, and revenue have not been disclosed.

The risks are equally clear. A 128-core chip must clear three hurdles: coherent interconnect, RAS, and the software stack. Its主打 “agent CPU” is a category not yet validated at scale, and whether customers will pay a premium for SMT4 can only be answered once the first systems ship. The capital-side signal, however, is unambiguous: from GPU strategic placements in 2025 to compute-in-memory and RISC-V CPUs in 2026, Meituan has placed bets on three separate lines.

Beijing Puts RISC-V Intelligent-Compute Chips on Its Embodied-Intelligence Research Agenda (11-11)

The Beijing Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology issued the “Beijing Embodied Intelligence Robot Industry High-Quality Development Action Plan (2026–2028).” The plan was finalized on November 11, and media interpretation spread widely from November 12. In its list of key core technology research items, the document names two categories of chips: RISC-V-based system-level intelligent-compute chips and high-dynamic-load N-channel MOSFETs, and calls for forward-looking布局 of ultra-low-power intelligent-compute chips. The accompanying phrasing is “lay out whatever should be laid out, adapt whatever can be adapted.”

Quantified targets include: by 2028, cultivate no fewer than 5 world-class and 10 domestically leading humanoid robot OEMs, with Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei accounting for 25% of national OEM capacity. Writing an instruction set architecture into the main text of a robot-specific policy is uncommon in China. The rationale can be reconstructed in three points: an open instruction set lets vendors harden vector, matrix, and dedicated operators into the pipeline; no licensing fees or royalties make a real difference for robot chips shipping hundreds of thousands of units a year and highly sensitive to bill-of-materials cost; and supply-chain autonomy and controllability. Together, these three make RISC-V the only general-purpose architecture named in the document.

Policy language must be distinguished from engineering reality. The system-level metrics constrain the power and thermal budget of edge compute, and also explain why the document lists “ultra-low-power intelligent-compute chips” as a separate item: the compute inside a robot’s body must first live within that power envelope.

Five Matrix-Extension Proposals on One Stage: IME, VME, AME, and Two Kinds of Dot Product (11-15)

On November 15, FPRox published a survey of the state of matrix extensions, placing several routes under discussion at RISC-V International side by side on one table, categorized by three multiply-accumulate layouts: “dot product, fat inner product, outer product.” The proposals listed include: the four-element dot product for 8-bit integers Zvdot4a8i, and the Integrated Matrix Extension IME (Zvv*mm) advanced in a separate repository. There is also VME (Zvt) under the vector extension, the standalone Attached Matrix Extension AME (Ztt), and the batched dot product Zv[b]dota. Specification status is as of mid-November 2026 and will be updated as drafts change.

The difference among the three layouts is not whether they can compute, but arithmetic intensity and register pressure. Dot product is the most flexible—vector-by-matrix and matrix-by-matrix can both be decomposed into dot products—but each computation requires moving a linear number of elements, giving it the lowest arithmetic intensity. Fat inner product is closest to classical matrix multiplication and suits matrix-by-matrix, but has low utilization for vector-by-matrix. Outer product compresses the inner-product dimension to near 1; as long as the accumulator stays resident and the k dimension is large enough, it trades linear loads for quadratic computation. It has the highest arithmetic intensity but the most demanding operand-shape requirements.

These differences determine three things: whether to add a new register file or reuse vector registers, how to arrange the memory layout, and whether the compiler can automatically map existing operators onto it.

For the industry, the real risk is not the number of proposals but their incompatibility. IME uses a separate matrix register file and separate instructions, VME attaches to vector registers, and AME is yet another attachment scheme. If they do not converge on a single mainline, operator libraries and compiler backends will have to maintain a path for every combination. RISC-V’s selling point of “one codebase running on many chips” for AI inference would be weakened.

Domestic activity this week corroborates this line. Nuclei’s NI1000 in the previous issue chose the VME route, while SpacemiT’s BMC first addressed cache coherence rather than matrices. Different vendors picking routes for their own scenarios is reasonable in the short term; in the long term, a common baseline is needed.

Including IME1 means older platforms like K1 can run models of this class. Parallel repack addresses load time: the number of expert weights in MoE models grows linearly with scale, and single-threaded repack can drag startup to the minute level.

On November 19, the same branch performed a larger upstream sync, merging “Sync upstream llama.cpp master into mtmd-backend,” plus a commit adapting precision enums. This sync exposed the structure of maintenance cost. The RISC-V backend modifies ggml’s tensor operators and memory layout, and upstream is rapidly refactoring this part. When a low-level type like the precision enum changes, every downstream backend must recompile and verify semantics. SpacemiT’s approach is to keep a long-lived branch and sync in periodic batches. The benefit is that merge conflicts are handled in one place; the cost is that each sync requires a batch of adaptation commits.

On November 17, SpacemiT’s model compression toolchain xslim pushed five commits in a row on the agent branch, all centered on quantization strategy. They cover selecting per-layer calibration methods based on user data, parsing and validating a unified precision plan, validating fusion provenance and effective precision boundaries, budgeted automatic mixed precision, and task-metric search with controlled local fp16. The direction is to turn quantization from hand-written configuration into search: given a precision budget and a task metric, let the tool decide which layers use which precision.

The recurring provenance and fused validation suggests they have been burned on real models by fused-operator precision mismatches. Quantized convolutions get fused by the backend, and the precision actually in effect is not what the config file says. Without validation, you get silent drift where “the config says int8 but it runs as fp16.” The agent branch naming is also worth noting: the toolchain is beginning to organize its code and skills around “for use by automated agents.”

Academia: Hypnos Splits Continual Learning into “Awake” and “Sleep” Phases (11-19)

On November 19, IoT Tech News reported on the Hypnos platform from Delft University of Technology, with the paper posted to arXiv in the same period (2611.24637). The problem this platform addresses: letting edge devices continue learning new samples after deployment without sending raw data to a server.

Hypnos splits learning into two phases, corresponding to the brain’s awake and sleep states. The awake phase runs on-device, centered on an X-HEEP RISC-V microcontroller plus a Quadrilatero matrix coprocessor, with an additional compact accelerator handling depthwise separable convolutions, per-channel requantization, and prototype operations. This phase performs only gradient-free prototype updates; backpropagation does not run on-device. The sleep phase is delegated to a smart charger: the charger retrieves product-quantization codes and weights from the device, decodes hidden-layer activations, performs gradient updates via replay, and writes the consolidated parameters back.

Metrics per the paper. On the CORe50 NICv2-391 protocol, the INT8 activation configuration reaches 78.6% accuracy with 7.79 seconds per-sample on-device latency; the INT16 configuration trades up to 81.6% at 10.54 seconds. Compared with quantized hidden-layer replay, it uses 3.8× fewer learning steps and 22× less per-sample learning compute. The 28nm synthesis result is 9.43 mm² and 33.3 mW at 375MHz. The awake-phase accelerator occupies only 10.7% of area, and the 5.48MB replay buffer sits on the charger side. The entire design is open-sourced.

What is worth noting is the resource-scheduling approach. It does not try to cram training into a battery-powered device; instead it moves the “consolidation” step to moments when wall power and large memory are available. The cost is that the device must be plugged in to form long-term memory; the benefit is that on-device power and area are pushed into a usable range. The limitation is also clear: 7.79 seconds per sample only suits slow-changing sensor streams, and camera-grade continual learning is still far off.

VI. Trend Observations

  • Mass production is landing on “secondary-core” positions — this is strategy, not retreat: This week’s three mass-production or delivery announcements all point to the same class of position — StarFive’s BMC, Zijing’s body-control MCU, and Infineon’s automotive MCU family. What they share: every unit ships with one, compute requirements are moderate, and reliability and long-term supply demands are high. Breaking in at these positions keeps the validation cycle manageable, and once you’re in, it’s years of supply. This week there were no public cases of volume replacement in primary compute, showing that the real barrier remains the software stack and system-level integration, not the core’s peak performance.

  • A top-tier supplier overhauling a flagship family is the week’s heaviest architectural signal: Infineon holds more than one-third of the global automotive MCU market and is merging its TriCore and TRAVEO lines into a single generation of RISC-V products. The weight of this lies not in the specs but in its willingness to absorb migration costs. AUTOSAR configuration, debuggers, functional safety certification, and supply-chain re-validation all have to be redone. The price is time: no SKUs, no sample timeline, and production vehicles won’t arrive until around 2029. For domestic automotive-grade teams, this means a competitive window of another two to three years — but the opponent is already the global first tier.

  • Open-source cores entering a “verifiable, maintainable” phase says more about maturity than performance: XiangShan’s week was spent on memory-access queue boundary defects, ICache address translation, CSR naming refactoring, and switching difftest to a downstream Chisel while pinning mill. CORE-V worked on the vector memory-access skeleton, RVFI CSR logging, and AHB compliance. FURSOY delivered Sail differential testing together with synthesis scripts. Four projects, four paths, and every action lands on “can it run long-term, can others verify it.” For teams building products on open-source cores, this phase is actually a good time to engage: interfaces are stable and defects are being systematically cleaned up.

  • Distributions consolidating their maintenance surface determines whether they can still track upstream three years from now: openRuyi had 85 commits this week. The work: backporting Go’s SHA-2 AES optimization, switching gRPC from bundled BoringSSL to system OpenSSL 4, and pulling xgrammar and tvm-ffi into the repository. revyos pushed the kernel from “per-platform config changes” up to the Debian packaging layer. RISE turned Python wheel coverage into a trackable table and treats “retiring self-built builds once upstream takes over” as a success metric. None of the three is doing new features — they’re doing “who maintains this.” A large part of RISC-V’s software gap hides in this kind of daily alignment.

  • The matrix-extension path debate is starting to carry a cost: IME, VME, AME, and two dot-product proposals run in parallel, differing in register file, memory layout, and compiler mapping. This week XiangShan did matrix and AME validation in difftest, with AME and the matrix prefix accounting for all three of commits 11-19. Nuclei chose the VME path last issue, while StarFive on BMC first solved coherence rather than matrix. Vendors picking paths by scenario is reasonable short-term. Long-term, without a common baseline, operator libraries and compiler backends will have to maintain a path for every combination, and the “one codebase runs on many chips” selling point will be diluted.

  • Delivery timelines for systems and chips generally land in 2027-2029, with the two years in between as a software window: Purism points to delivery in 2028; Lingrui Zhixin points to tape-out in 2027 and mass production in 2028. FIORA5 points to a prototype in 2027 and licensing in 2029; Infineon RC1 points to hitting vehicles around 2029; Longqing Kongtian and SingNova gave no mass-production milestones. This batch of commitments all come due two years out or more. In the window in between, any problem in the software stack, certification, or supply chain will delay landing. Three things worth watching next: whether JH-B100’s quarterly shipments materialize, whether the K1 phone’s graphics stack runs on 2027 test devices, and the first tape-out results of a 128-core-class RISC-V server chip.
