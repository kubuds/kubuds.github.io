I. Weekly Highlights

DAMO XuanTie Releases XL-300 High-Throughput Interconnect IP: 8-Core Asynchronous, 32MB L3, 90% Linearity (11-12)

IT Home reported on November 12 that XuanTie, Alibaba DAMO Academy’s RISC-V brand, has released the XL-300 high-throughput cache-coherent interconnect IP design: it supports asynchronous connection of up to 8 cores, a 256-bit data width, an L3 Cache configurable up to 32MB, and 8-core concurrent linearity of over 90%, with a bandwidth ceiling roughly double that of the previous-generation XL-200, plus a new Direct Memory Transfer feature designed to bypass traditional paths and break through the throughput bottlenecks caused by large-capacity L3 and high bus latency. Phoenix Tech, Sina Tech, and MyDrivers republished the story the same day with identical content.

The weight of this news lies in the “interconnect IP” position. RISC-V’s progress in single-core performance and vector and matrix extensions is verifiable, but stitching multiple cores into a usable SoC depends on the cache coherence protocol, cross-core bandwidth and latency, and L3 capacity and partitioning strategy—precisely where domestic RISC-V server chips are most prone to post-silicon problems. 8-core concurrent linearity of 90% means the marginal gain of the eighth core is still nine-tenths that of the first, a figure that says more about the actual level of the coherence protocol and bus arbitration than peak bandwidth does.

The timeline needs to be laid out clearly to avoid reading an old product as a new one. The XL-300 is not making its first appearance: in a March 2026 in-depth analysis of the C930, XuanTie’s technical lead already listed it as the strongest interconnect IP paired with the flagship server core C930, with figures at the time of up to 8 cores per cluster, L3 up to 23MB, a 20% frequency increase and doubled bandwidth versus the XL-200, and only a 5% area increase. This week’s report brings a more complete public specification disclosure, but the difference in L3 capacity from 23MB to 32MB has not been explained officially; it may be different configuration tiers, or specifications raised over iterations. This publication records the two figures side by side as independent accounts.

Nuclei Releases NI1000: First RISC-V General-Purpose AI Compute Core Supporting the VME Matrix Extension (11-09)

Nuclei Technology released the NI1000 RISC-V CPU IP on November 9, which the company says is its first high-performance general-purpose AI processor IP supporting the Vector Matrix Extension (VME). The NI series can be configured as the 900 or 1000 series; the VLEN of the RVV 1.0 vector unit (VPU) can be set to 512, 1024, 2048, or 4096 bits, and each core in a cluster can independently configure its own VPU; cores are tightly coupled with NPU accelerators via IOCP while maintaining cache coherence, and the NICE and VNICE interfaces allow users to define custom scalar and vector instructions.

The technical point lies in how VME is implemented: VME is tightly coupled with the CPU and RVV within the same core, reusing the RVV vector register file for source operands and the RVV execution units for computation, adding only the area of the Matrix Tiles; the new native matrix instructions accumulate outer-product results into dedicated N×N accumulators, four independent Matrix Tiles run in ping-pong parallelism, and native FP8 support is added. Nuclei’s figures are: running a 32×32 FP32 matrix multiplication on the NI1020 (VLEN 1024), overall area increases by only 15% while matrix multiplication performance improves 2.5×.

The positioning is stated with restraint: the company makes clear that the NI series is not intended to replace accelerator IP such as NPUs and GPUs, but to combine with them. Compared with IME, which has independent matrix accumulation hardware and higher energy efficiency, and with AME, which eliminates a full independent matrix register file, VME sits in the middle, trading controllable hardware overhead for matrix compute, at the cost of lower peak energy efficiency than dedicated matrix units. The real barrier is not the IP but whether operator libraries and compilers can actually put VME to use.

Date: 2026-11-09　Source: Nuclei Technology press release, Design And Reuse report

Automotive-Grade RISC-V Completes Three Steps in Three Days: Product Definition, IP Source, Toolchain (11-09, 11-10)

After Infineon’s AURIX RC1 launched on November 4 (up to 12 cores, 40MB storage, ASIL D, sampling in the first half of 2027), two more links in the chain were filled this week. On November 9, a Design And Reuse reprint analysis pointed out that the RC1 uses MIPS processor IP and is fabricated by GlobalFoundries, while also raising the question of “whether RISC-V will replace Infineon’s own TriCore architecture”; Infineon’s official line has consistently been “the first RISC-V-based automotive MCU platform, with the TriCore family continuing to ship,” without elaborating on the IP source.

On November 10, two European automotive software tool vendors issued support statements on the same day, landing precisely on the opening day of electronica 2026 in Munich. HighTec’s approach is to fold the RC1’s device-specific compiler options into a single multi-architecture compiler, while providing safety- and security-certified C/C++ and Rust toolchains, with support extending from AURIX to TRAVEO and PSOC; TASKING, meanwhile, folds the entire lifecycle into one tool suite—VX-toolset for compilation, winIDEA and BlueBox for debugging, LDRA for verification—with built-in Agentic AI, on a timeline of compiler availability in December 2026 and toolchain certification completed in 2027.

Reading the three days of news together, the sequence for automotive-grade RISC-V is IP, silicon, toolchain, certification, with clear external signals at each of the four steps, and the two tool vendors’ certification timing aligned with Infineon’s sampling timing. For teams working on domestic automotive-grade RISC-V, what deserves attention in this round is not that there are two more compiler vendors, but that “certification timelines” are becoming the standard format of customer questions—who provides the functional safety evidence chain directly determines whether a project can get off the ground.

Beijing Issues Three-Year Embodied Intelligence Action Plan: 200,000 Units Annual Capacity and a 50,000 PFlops Cluster (11-11)

The Beijing Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology issued the “Beijing Embodied Intelligence Robot Industry High-Quality Development Action Plan (2026–2028)” on November 11, replacing directional language with assessable numbers: by 2028, cultivate no fewer than 5 world-class and 10 domestically leading humanoid robot OEMs; the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region’s OEM capacity to account for 25% of the national total, with key component localization exceeding 60%; form an annual OEM production capacity of 200,000 units in Beijing; and newly build embodied intelligence dedicated compute clusters with a total scale exceeding 50,000 PFlops.

The manufacturing-side targets are written hard: rated payload of 40% or more of the robot’s own weight, mean time between failures exceeding 2,000 hours, annual failure rate of core motion components and end effectors below 10%, design service life exceeding 5 years, continuous-operation battery life of more than 4 hours, and the cost of a single high-DOF dexterous hand reduced to under RMB 10,000. On the governance side, the plan proposes “one code per machine” full-process management, a “one-on-one” service mechanism for OEM listings, and explicitly opposes involution-style competition.

For the RISC-V line, the plan’s value lies in turning the demand side from a vision into procurement parameters: the joint side needs hard real-time, low power, and deterministic networking, while the body side needs compute and bandwidth capable of running VLA inference, and both must be mass-produced under a 2,000-hour failure-free constraint. Domestic supply that currently matches these two tiers includes the SpacemiT K3 series, the Sophgo SG and BM series, Nuclei’s N1000 and NI1000, and joint-level real-time cores such as the MIPS Atlas M8500. The plan itself names no instruction set or supplier; the above correspondences are a comparison against the current state of the supply side.

Distributions Converge Their Platform Matrices in the Same Week: RevyOS Covers Six Platforms with One defconfig, openRuyi Kernel Bumped Twice (11-09 to 11-13)

The RevyOS linux repository did two things this week that differ in weight but point in the same direction. In a single push of 20 commits in the early hours of November 9, it added debian_defconfig, folding the configuration items of six platforms—ESWIN EIC7700, UltraRISC DP1000, SpacemiT K3 and K1, T-Head TH1520, and Sophgo SG2042 and SG2044—into a single defconfig, and added CI for both cross-architecture and native builds; on November 11, another 101 commits landed in a single window, filling in item by item the K3’s GPU node and display power domain, USB3 PHY, and UFS WriteBooster, the EIC7700’s fan controller and thermal zones, and the device trees for the SG2044’s SRD3-10-LB and SRD3-12 boards.

The openRuyi main repository saw four consecutive days of high-density rolling: on 11-08 the kernel was bumped to 7.3.3 and LTS 6.18.62, glibc to 2.44 with the full test suite run on x86_64; 11-09 to 11-10 brought twenty routine upgrades (wlroots 0.20.2, wget2 2.3.0, Python bindings for llvm22 through llvm24 switched to explicit linking against libclang); 11-10 moved into a security backport batch (policycoreutils 3.11, cups, lxc, and libmtp backporting upstream patches, libssh 0.12.2); and 11-11’s thirty-six commits closed out OpenTelemetry and four Python authentication packages together, bumped the kernel to 7.3.4 and 6.18.63, and restricted the XTHEADVECTOR and XLINUXMEMALIAS options to the rva20 platform.

Neither is doing new features; both are about “whether others can compile it and dare put it into production.” The long-standing problem for RISC-V distributions is one config and one patch set per board, so upgrading the kernel means redoing the adaptation; folding six platforms into one defconfig and bringing security fixes into the mainline lowers downstream costs more than shipping another release. The official website still shows no new version this week—the openRuyi news page’s latest entries remain the two from October 2026 (Ruyi Firmware, ecosystem infrastructure), and the November release has not yet shipped.

T-Head Spinoff Heads to Hong Kong: From IPO Rumors to Alibaba Announcement and HKEX Confirmation (11-09 to 11-11)

On November 9, multiple media outlets citing Reuters reported that T-Head Semiconductor plans to list on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange in 2027, targeting a fundraising of US$2 billion to US$3 billion. On November 10, Hong Kong’s Hong Kong Economic Times further reported that T-Head had confidentially submitted a listing application to the HKEX; on the evening of November 10, Alibaba issued an announcement of its plan to spin off wholly owned subsidiary T-Head for a separate listing on the HKEX Main Board, with the HKEX having confirmed that Alibaba may proceed with the spin-off, and T-Head having submitted its A1 application confidentially as early as September 11. The announcement also left blanks: neither the offering size nor the reduction in Alibaba’s shareholding has been determined, and because the highest applicable percentage ratio is below 5%, the spin-off does not even constitute a disclosable transaction for Alibaba.

Valuation and business figures come in multiple versions: fundraising of US$2 billion to US$3 billion, with valuation ranges from US$20 billion to US$30 billion given by one institutional source, to JPMorgan’s rough January benchmarking estimate of US$25 billion to US$62 billion; on the business side, as of April 2026 the Zhenwu series AI chips had cumulative shipments of 560,000 units, serving more than 650 enterprise customers, with external commercial customers accounting for over 60%; the Zhenwu V900 has 216GB of memory, 1,200GB/s inter-chip bandwidth, can connect up to 500,000 chips into a cluster, and is scheduled for mass production in Q1 2027.

The relationship with RISC-V needs restating, because this framing has been repeated by the media for several rounds: the listing entity is T-Head, whose product focus is PPU (AI accelerator cards) and Yitian (Arm server CPUs); the XuanTie RISC-V processor IP was spun off from T-Head in 2024 and transferred to DAMO Academy, and does only RISC-V processors. Media roundups often lump in “XuanTie’s cumulative shipments of 6 billion units,” but that is a group-level historical figure and does not mean the listed assets include the RISC-V IP business. What really determines the valuation are two splits in the prospectus: how much of the tens-of-billions-scale annualized revenue comes from supplying Alibaba Cloud versus procurement outside the group, and whether internal settlement is at fair value.

Three Purchasable Form Factors Emerge on the System Side: Router, Desktop AI System, and Open-Source SoC (11-12)

Start9 released a RISC-V router on November 12, built jointly with Deepin, powered by the SpacemiT K1 (eight-core 64-bit RISC-V), running StartWRT based on OpenWrt, priced at US$300, configured with 4GB LPDDR4, 16GB eMMC, a replaceable Wi-Fi 6E miniPCIe module, and one gigabit WAN and one gigabit LAN port. The official page spells out item by item “how open it is”: the K1 reference schematic, OpenSBI and U-Boot, the Linux kernel and mt76 driver, and the MIT-licensed StartWRT are all open; the wireless RF firmware and the two early-stage binaries executed at power-on are not open, with the latter being replaced. This way of “listing each part that cannot be open-sourced” is more credible than a blanket claim of full open source.

SingNova (Nanyang Star Technology) exhibited four RISC-V-based products at Safety & Security Asia 2026 in Singapore, among them AI Power Studio, a 12-core RISC-V plus dataflow architecture with a claimed maximum of 256 TOPS (INT8), up to 128GB of LPDDR5 ECC unified memory, and up to 128 channels of 1080p decoding; its video storage server emphasizes chip-level intelligent encoding, claiming to cut storage requirements to one-third of the original. What needs restraint is that this is a trade-show press release, 256 TOPS is a vendor-claimed figure without test conditions such as batch size, model, or whether sparsity is included, and the specific microarchitecture of the 12 cores is not disclosed.

On the same day, Hackaday reported on Bunnie Huang’s Baochip-1x: a 22nm-process, “mostly open” SoC for high-assurance applications, with an MMU and capable of running RV32IMAC software, specified as a single-core 350MHz VexRISC-V, 4MB resistive memory plus 2MB SRAM, with SystemVerilog and Verilog source open, and a companion “Dabao” evaluation board shaped much like a Raspberry Pi Pico. Its competitive dimension is not performance and cost, but “every transistor can be accounted for”; in scenarios with tightening supply-chain scrutiny, “being able to read the RTL” matters more than a high benchmark score.

II. Instruction Set and Architecture Ecosystem

XiangShan Kunminghu V3: 18 TLB Refactoring Commits in One Pass, Debug Fields Stripped from ROB (11-10 to 11-12)

The kunminghu-v3 branch of the main XiangShan repo saw activity on each of three days this week. On 11-10, five commits: changed the VBTB prediction selection logic in MBTB to taken-first, fixed NewCSR not re-fetching when a CSR write changes breakpoint gating, preserved ROB pending-slot reset during redirect cleanup, aligned CBO invalidate events to cache line granularity, plus one codeowners maintenance commit. On 11-11, twenty-one commits, of which 18 were a refactor(TLB) series with the same day and same timestamp, none changing behavior — all structural governance: naming fields for TLB read results and PTW bypass results, sharing dynamic VPN index selection, extracting permission and PBMT checks into the TLB source, aggregating access-check information by port, and removing unused scaffolding such as blocking TLB support and the saveLevel configuration. On 11-12, three commits turned to observability: introduced RobSlotInfo, keeping only the three fields commitType, fuType, and fuOpType in normal RTL while stripping the entire debug uop copy; wired up the topdown counters for the floating-point issue queue and ROB; and fixed JumpUnit not assigning isRVC on backend redirect, which caused incorrect length determination for compressed jump instructions.

All three threads point to the same judgment: being able to do a “structure-only, behavior-unchanged” batch refactor of a module presupposes a sufficiently dense regression surface and stable behavior. TLB and PTW are the hardest-to-debug stretch of the memory access path; converging permission checks and bypass results to a single exit will shrink the change surface considerably when adding extensions like Svnapot and Svpbmt later. The topdown counters, meanwhile, turn “why is the pipeline idling” from a matter of experience into attributable data.

Date: 2026-11-10 to 2026-11-12　Source: OpenXiangShan/XiangShan, OpenXiangShan/difftest

NEMU: Matrix Extension Moves from “Semantic Completion” to “Memory Access Fast Path” (11-10, 11-11)

NEMU had six commits across two windows this week, splitting neatly into correctness and performance groups. On 11-10, fixed riscv64-side writes to SIP latching timer interrupts (#1242), a detail error on the interrupt virtualization path; the same day AME switched to CRC32C for computing the matrix completion hash (#1280). On 11-11, four commits: added a PF refresh enable bit to SMBLOCKCTL (#1285), turning this control bit from reserved into configurable behavior; fixed on the RVV side zero-length gather, slide, and mask-class operations not setting the dirty VS bit (#1286) — a missed vector state dirty bit would cause the host to misjudge whether context needs saving in virtualization scenarios; fixed on the MMU side that a non-canonical address on the second page should report the next page base; and on the performance side enabled fast paths for matrix load and store in the shared ref (#1284).

AME is the XiangShan-family matrix extension, and over the past year NEMU’s support for it has gone from completing instruction semantics to optimizing the memory access path — indicating the matrix unit has run enough in the simulator that performance has become the bottleneck. The same day, two supporting commits on the difftest side: removed the latest tag from the Firtool release in the nightly workflow (#1040), and vectorized the MMA CPU check vectors while reusing already-decoded operands (#1038). The focus of verification infrastructure is shifting from “is it functionally correct” to “does it run fast enough.”

CORE-V CVW: After Removing Two Local Branch Predictors, Turning to Verifiability Closure (11-07, 11-09)

CORE-V Wally (cvw) merged 20 commits in a batch on 11-07, in three groups: removed the BP_LOCAL_AHEAD and BP_LOCAL_REPAIR local branch predictors and the BTB_ADDR_BITS parameter; fixed centered PWM sticky and deglitch behavior, APB read strobe, and AHB/APB bridge tests; documented RV32GC CoreMark/MHz of 4.33 in the README and extracted the common CoreMark compile options for reuse. On 11-09 to 11-10, 11 more commits merged, with the main thread becoming verifiability: added LSR error-bit behavior to the 16550 UART, made SPI read back zero when the receive FIFO is empty, added exclusions to branch-prediction redirect coverage statistics, gave lockstep mode an exemption rule for unaligned accesses, and removed the SHA Questa script and Docker regression script.

Removing predictors is unusual on a teaching/research-oriented core; it usually means the team is moving toward “simple structure, easy timing closure, explainable results” rather than chasing IPC. Combined with the CoreMark/MHz 4.33 published the same week and this round of protocol detail closure, CVW is putting both “reproducible scores” and “synthesizable RTL” on the table at once — more practical for teams using it for teaching, pre-tapeout verification, and CORE-V ecosystem chip prototypes.

Date: 2026-11-07, 2026-11-09　Source: openhwfoundation/cvw

Toolchain and Debug Visibility: Instruction-Level Trace, RISC-V-only clang, and LLVM Python Bindings (11-09 to 11-12)

Espressif pushed esp-riscv-trace to 0.2.0. This tool decodes hardware instruction traces captured on Espressif RISC-V chips, reading captures produced by OpenOCD or a core ELF with trace annotations, reconstructing program execution flow, resolving each PC to a function and source line, and outputting hotspot tables, pre-crash call history, or call stacks directly feedable to flame graphs, marking continuity losses as gaps rather than guessing to fill them. 0.2.0 adds four commands — decode, profile, stats, history — pushing this capability from “can capture” to “can analyze,” supporting ESP32-C5, C6, C61, H2, H21, H4, P4, and S31.

The community-organized CH32 RISC-V User Group released ch32rv-clang: a clang and lld built from upstream LLVM with only the RISCV backend retained, packaged as one archive per host system for ArduinoCore-CH32RV to install via the board manager, supporting WCH Qingke’s XW compressed instructions across five host platforms. It deliberately subtracts — not bundling the C library or compiler runtime — in exchange for an unzip-and-use install path, eliminating half of the most common deterrent: “can’t get the toolchain installed.”

On the openRuyi side, a class of “installs but doesn’t work” problems was fixed: the llvm22, llvm23, and llvm24 packages use sed to change clang’s Python bindings to link against libclang, preventing users on RISC-V from failing at the import stage when installing llvmlite or triton; on 11-12 llvm23 was bumped to 23.1.5. Nuclei’s nuclei-tool-guide had 12 commits in a single day breaking down known issues in the 2026.10 release by component, and on 11-11 added documentation for GDB live variable watch — debug experience has long been a weak spot in the RISC-V MCU ecosystem, and updates like these retain customers better than shipping new cores.

Date: 2026-11-09 to 2026-11-12　Source: espressif/esp-riscv-trace, Adafruit coverage, openRuyi-Project/openRuyi, Nuclei-Software/nuclei-tool-guide

Teaching and Open-Source Cores: Specula’s Out-of-Order Core, a Computer on Tang Nano 9K, Baochip-1x (11-06 to 11-12)

Developer Pranav published a long post on 11-06 about Specula, a dual-issue out-of-order RV32I processor he implemented from scratch in Bluespec SystemVerilog, breaking down register renaming and retirement, reservation stations and issue, store forwarding and speculation, and how comparison trees and register ports squeeze timing, item by item. The value of such content isn’t in code volume but in pulling the out-of-order core’s barrier out of the black box of papers and commercial IP — teams in China doing RISC-V teaching and pre-tapeout verification have long lacked an intermediate textbook “from in-order to out-of-order.”

The m9b project that appeared on Hacker News on 11-09 is another kind of layered sample: a complete small computer built on a roughly twenty-dollar Sipeed Tang Nano 9K using the VexiiRiscv soft core — 36MHz, 8MB PSRAM, 720×480 HDMI, 48kHz audio, two USB ports for keyboard and mouse, SD card for program storage, capable of running Doom (timedemo at about 22 fps), Descent (about 10 fps), Tyrian 2.1 (full speed at 35 fps), and five other DOS-era games. The project splits the SoC, runtime, five game ports, and SD card image into separate repositories, all open source, each documenting where it modified upstream.

Together with Baochip-1x on 11-12, the three threads show that RISC-V’s “non-high-performance” branch still has real demand: reproducible teaching cores, FPGA projects that make clear “what a computer minimally needs,” and high-assurance SoCs with auditable designs. None of these three produce benchmark scores, but they determine how newcomers and new scenarios enter this ecosystem.

Date: 2026-11-06 to 2026-11-12　Source: Specula long post, m9b-system repo, Hacker News discussion, Hackaday coverage

III. Operating Systems and Distributions

The official news page remains stuck on two posts from October 2026; the November release has not shipped, so the above counts only as repo-side progress.

IV. Chips and Hardware

XuanTie XL-300 and the XT-Link Three-Generation Line: From “Can It Connect” to “Throughput Under a Large L3” (11-12)

Lining up public information from past years reveals the iteration cadence of XuanTie’s interconnect IP. In July 2024, XL100 was released as the first multi-Cluster interconnect IP supporting cache coherency, positioned as a processor scaling solution for 8 to 32 cores with support for big.LITTLE configurations; at the XuanTie Ecosystem Conference in February 2025, XL200 appeared in the R&D roadmap, described as “providing larger-scale, higher-performance multi-cluster coherent interconnect,” with no specific parameters at the time; in the March 2026 in-depth analysis of the C930, XL-300 was already the strongest interconnect IP paired with the C930, stated as up to 8 cores per Cluster, L3 up to 23MB, a 20% frequency increase and doubled bandwidth versus XL-200, with only a 5% area increase; this issue’s report gives 8-core asynchronous connection, 256bit, 32MB L3, linearity above 90%, doubled bandwidth, and Direct Memory Transfer.

Three points warrant verification: the L3 capacity spans from 23MB to 32MB—nearly 40%, unlikely a typo, possibly a difference between single-Cluster and multi-Cluster configurations or an upper limit under different SRAM libraries; “20% frequency increase” appears in the March statement but not in this issue, while this issue adds “8-core concurrent linearity above 90%”—the former is a process and physical implementation metric, the latter an architecture and protocol efficiency metric; “asynchronous connection” means cores can run in different clock domains, which is necessary when big and little cores are mixed and cores and IO belong to different power domains, but it raises coherency protocol complexity.

Organizational corroboration: a 168-hour check shows the XuanTie organization (XUANTIE-RV) had only two pushes within the window, qemu (11-10) and the newly appeared zvfx-proposal repo (11-12 20:20). The latter is dated the same day as the XL-300 report, but its default branch has no readable commits or description, so no technical interpretation is offered; product-level new releases this week were zero, and XL-300 is an external specification disclosure rather than new silicon.

Nuclei NI1000’s Implementation Trade-offs: RVV as the Base with Matrix Added on Demand (11-09)

NI1000’s technical route is worth viewing side by side with the other two matrix extension paths: IME has dedicated matrix accumulation hardware and higher matrix compute energy efficiency; AME omits a full dedicated matrix register file and hardware matrix accelerator; VME sits in the middle, reusing RVV for both source operands and compute units, adding only Matrix Tiles, trading controllable hardware overhead for matrix compute. Nuclei also judges that VME will become the earliest-standardized RISC-V matrix extension—whether this holds depends on how fast toolchains and operator libraries follow, not on the IP itself.

On product cadence, IP release is only the beginning. Nuclei’s nuclei-tool-guide had 12 commits in a single day on 11-09, all for the 2026.10 version: upgrade notes, QEMU and OpenOCD release notes, libncrt fix entries split by version, and IDE known issues, plus pushing the VS Code extension to 0.3.0; on 11-11 it added GDB real-time variable watching. Such a detailed known-issues list indicates the version has entered customer delivery—for an IP vendor, this kind of engineering investment retains customers better than shipping another core.

Automotive-Grade and Security Cores: AURIX RC1’s Supply Chain, AMD Zen 6’s RISC-V TEE (11-06 to 11-10)

Jamestown Foundation Assesses China’s RISC-V: Strategy Ahead of Capability (11-10)

On November 10, U.S. think tank the Jamestown Foundation published an article assessing China’s RISC-V progress, authored by Sunny Cheung. The article presents several verifiable factual threads: SpacemiT and the XiangShan team unveiled the 46-core server chip V100 at the 6th RISC-V China Summit on October 19, claiming it to be the first general-purpose chip fully compliant with the RISC-V server standard; the “15th Five-Year Plan for the Electronic Information Manufacturing Industry” released in September proposed advancing RISC-V R&D and industrialization and developing RISC-V chips for artificial intelligence—previously, national-level policy documents rarely named the instruction set directly.

The article’s core judgment is “strategy over capability”: expanded use and influence over international organizations are clear, but performance still lags proprietary chips; the article cites concerns from Chinese analysts about a “glass ceiling” and states that RISC-V chips’ share of substitution in the national IT substitution plan is “negligible.” Such external assessments are worth reading as a cross-reference—they test the domestically common claim that “the ecosystem is already established” against a different set of metrics, and the gap between the two sets of metrics is precisely the question domestic RISC-V must answer in the next phase.

VI. Trend Observations

  • Interconnect IP has moved from supporting role to the main thread of RISC-V serverization: Among the specs disclosed this cycle for XL-300, the most telling item is not the 32MB L3 but the 8-core concurrency linearity of over 90%—a metric only someone who has actually run multi-core workloads would dare to give. Single-core performance depends on microarchitecture, multi-core performance on coherence and bus; domestic RISC-V can already hold a conversation at the SPECint2006 15/GHz level on single-core, and the deciding factor over the next two to three years will fall on multi-core scaling efficiency and post-silicon stability. The one-generation-per-year cadence from XL100 to XL300 shows this line is being run as a product.

  • The competitive point for automotive-grade has shifted to toolchains and certification timing: The three steps of AURIX RC1 over three days—11-04 product definition, 11-09 IP source, 11-10 toolchain support—lay out clearly the sequence for advancing automotive-grade RISC-V: chip vendors define the platform, IP vendors supply the core, tool vendors provide compilation and debugging, and certification comes last. The “tools available in December, certification in 2027” given by the two tool vendors dovetails exactly with Infineon’s “sampling in the first half of 2027,” indicating the timeline was coordinated. The question domestic teams will most likely be asked next is not “do you have a RISC-V core” but “when will your toolchain pass certification.”

  • Distributions are shifting from “one board, one config” to “platform matrix plus reproducible CI”: A single debian_defconfig in RevyOS covers six platforms, and 101 commits in a single day consolidate board support into combinations; openRuyi over four consecutive days did version upgrades, security backports, kernel tagging, and firmware integration, and on Friday pushed kernel-team-tools tags to 7.3.4-3 and 6.18.63-4. Neither is shipping new features; both are about “whether others can build it and dare put it into production.” This lowers downstream costs more than shipping one more release.

  • Verification and observability have become the new front line of toolchain competition: NEMU adds a fast path for AME matrix memory access, difftest vectorizes MMA verification vectors and removes the tee pipeline that produced false successes, XiangShan wires topdown counters into the ROB, and Espressif turns instruction-level trace into four pip-installable commands—four actions pointing to the same judgment: once cores and extensions reach a certain complexity, the bottleneck shifts from “is the design correct” to “can we quickly see what it is doing.” Teams underinvesting in this line will pay multiplied time in the integration phase.

  • Demand-side policy is quantified, writing compute tiers into procurement parameters: The Beijing plan gives 200,000 units of annual complete-machine capacity, a 60% localization rate for key components, dexterous hands under RMB 10,000, and 2,000 hours mean time between failures—essentially writing “which chip, what cost, what reliability” into acceptance conditions. The joint side needs hard real-time and deterministic networking, the body side needs compute and bandwidth to run VLA, and the training side needs 50,000 PFlops clusters—none of these three tiers can be solved by a single big chip. MIPS’s three-tier reference designs and SingNova’s single-box solution represent exactly two answers, and the watershed is where software maintenance costs are paid.

  • Capital and external assessments are simultaneously anchoring domestic RISC-V: T-Head’s spin-off and Hong Kong listing puts a comparable target on the table—”already commercialized, with external customers, with revenue”—while the Jamestown Foundation uses another set of metrics—single-core performance, software maturity, and actual substitution rate—to conclude that “strategy precedes capability.” Both point to the same homework: when the narrative is dominated by shipment volumes, summit scale, and standards compliance, the external revenue breakdown and measured performance data in the prospectus are what will truly be tested in the next stage.
