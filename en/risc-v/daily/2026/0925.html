Today’s Highlight: UPC Study: Using XuanTie C910 and On-Chip Performance Counters to Make RISC-V Chips “Self-Detect” Microarchitectural Attacks, with 99% Detection Accuracy

Date: 2026-09-24　Sources: Scienmag, “Machine Learning Turns RISC-V Chips Into Their Own Hardware Attack Detectors”, Paper: Pou, Otero, Robledo, Pajuelo & Canal, RISC-V hardware attack detection using on-chip hardware performance counters, Cybersecurity 9(1) Article 222, HARPY-V dataset (CORA Research Data Repository)

On September 24, Scienmag published a report on research by a team at the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya (UPC) in Spain: the researchers “installed” machine learning detectors into the RISC-V chip’s own hardware performance counters (HPCs), enabling real-time detection of microarchitectural attacks inside the chip. The paper was published in the journal Cybersecurity (9(1), Article 222). The experiments were completed on real commercial silicon — DAMO XuanTie C910 (a 64-bit out-of-order core, RV64GCV) mounted on a Sipeed LicheePi 4A development board running Debian 13; the team built and released the HARPY-V dataset: approximately 64,000 labeled samples, containing 16 types of microarchitectural attacks (Flush+Reload, Flush+Flush, Evict+Reload, Spectre V1/V2/RSB, timing drift, TLB and page-table walk attacks, as well as GhostWrite, among others) and 16 benign programs. The benign set deliberately uses workloads that share hardware structures with the attacks, bringing the classification problem close to real-world difficulty.

Results and engineering details: binary detection (attack/benign) achieved 99% accuracy, while multiclass classification (distinguishing specific attack types) reached about 95%; in a “leave-one-attack-out” zero-day simulation, combinations of random forest with mRMR feature selection and decision tree with RFECV still maintained high detection rates, and “choosing the right counters” was more critical than “stacking more counters.” The team also disclosed two valuable empirical lessons: on the C910, monitoring multiple counters simultaneously with perf causes mutual interference (some readings were amplified by more than 3x), so counters must be collected individually one at a time; and most of the C910’s perf event descriptions are inaccurate, so the counter mappings in the official manual must be treated as authoritative.

Significance and boundaries: this is one of the first microarchitectural attack detection studies to use commercial RISC-V silicon (rather than FPGA soft cores or simulators) as its platform and to release a reproducible dataset. “Using the chip’s built-in counters plus a lightweight model for proactive detection” is a low-cost hardening path that requires no silicon changes and no kernel patches, echoing the security narrative of the open RISC-V ecosystem; at the same time, its boundaries should be noted — the experiments cover only a single core model, counter patterns are strongly correlated with microarchitecture and require retraining across chips; supervised learning depends on labeled attack data, and generalization to entirely new attack types remains to be validated.

II. Product and Ecosystem Updates

2.1 Linux porting momentum builds for Espressif ESP32-S31: from MCU toward SBC form factor

Date: 2026-09-24　Source: XDA, “The newest ESP32 can actually run Linux, and it’s getting uncomfortably close to a Raspberry Pi”, CNX Software: ESP32-S31 Linux BSP developer preview (08-22)

XDA published a feature discussing Espressif’s ESP32-S31: a dual-core 32-bit RISC-V main core (up to 320 MHz, plus a 40 MHz low-power core) and the first ESP32 with a built-in Sv32 MMU. On the hardware side it also offers a Gigabit Ethernet MAC (RGMII), USB 2.0 High-Speed OTG host, dual-slot SDIO, DVP camera input, and a parallel LCD controller — a peripheral mix approaching that of a modern single-board computer. The official Linux BSP developer preview shipped in August (Buildroot 2025.02 + U-Boot + Linux 6.18 branch, full image about 13.7MB); community ports are advancing in parallel: GrieferPig’s Linux 6.12 supervisor-mode port (about 15MB usable memory, CoreMark around 1059), and another port based on Linux 7.1 + OpenSBI 1.9 that can drive an 800x480 LCD console. XDA’s assessment: the S31’s peripherals, bandwidth, and CPU privilege model are all “close to an SBC,” but Linux support remains experimental and is not recommended for production in the near term.

2.2 New Sophgo BM1688 board OrangePi O1 announced: 16 TOPS TPU plus RISC-V C906 co-processor

Date: 2026-09-24　Source: LinuxGizmos, “16-TOPS AI SBC with SATA 3.0 runs on octa-core Sophgo BM1688”

LinuxGizmos published details of the OrangePi O1 board (90×70mm): based on the Sophgo BM1688 vision computing processor — 8-core Arm Cortex-A53 (up to 1.65GHz) plus a RISC-V C906 co-processor (up to 1.2GHz), with an integrated TPU delivering INT8 16 TOPS / INT4 32 TOPS / FP16-BF16 around 4 TFLOPS, supporting Caffe, PyTorch, TensorFlow, ONNX, and MXNet, positioned for edge-side large-model and vision-model inference. On-board configuration: 8/16GB LPDDR4X, eMMC (16-128GB options), M.2 NVMe (PCIe 3.0), SATA 3.0, dual Gigabit Ethernet ports, Wi-Fi 6E / Bluetooth 5.3, optional 4G/5G modules, HDMI 2.0 4K60, dual MIPI-CSI and 6 video inputs; supports 16-channel 1080p30 decoding. The BM1688’s embedded RISC-V C906 co-processor is the “RISC-V inside” this Arm-main-core edge AI chip, and a typical form of the multi-core heterogeneous AI chip path taken by domestic RISC-V vendors.

2.3 Radxa Linkr v1.5.0 released: plugin center, ZeroTier / EasyTier network plugins, and screenshot OCR

Date: 2026-09-24　Source: Linkr v1.5.0 release notes

Radxa Linkr released v1.5.0: a new plugin center (one-stop management for uploading, installing, enabling, disabling, and uninstalling plugins); for networking, new ZeroTier and EasyTier network plugins (join a network, view network information, leave a network); adaptive resizing of screenshot watermarks and screenshot OCR text recognition. Linkr is Radxa’s browser-based remote management product line for development boards and NAS devices, and has been part of this daily’s routine monitoring scope since September; this is the major version update within the window.

III. Deep Dive on Domestic Teams

3.1 SpacemiT: llama.cpp fork releases v0.2.0, standalone ggml-spacemit backend debuts

Date: 2026-09-24　Source: spacemit-com/llama.cpp v0.2.0 release notes

The SpacemiT llama.cpp fork has released v0.2.0: the core change is the introduction of a standalone ggml-spacemit backend (build flag -DGGML_SPACEMIT=ON) — a single main thread submits tasks to the spine runtime, which then spawns worker threads, so the thread parameter -t is always set to 1; compute cores are specified via the environment variable SPACEMIT_PERFER_CORE_ID as a list of physical core IDs (e.g., cores 8 through 15), and models are placed via –device SPACEMIT0, with llama-cli/llama-bench/llama-server all scheduled this way. The server retains multi-stream ASR capability: it can pair with the smt media backend to handle audio requests concurrently (e.g., -np 4 with 6 cores). This fork has evolved continuously since the MTMD refactor in v0.1.7; this release lands “backend independence,” marking a milestone version in SpacemiT’s inference stack’s march toward modularization.

Sipeed’s SLogic logic analyzer software stack advanced 7 commits during the window, with the main thrust being the migration of the sipeed-slogic driver from a private implementation to the shared libslogic library (with all-logic upgraded in sync), unified builds of ngscope and sigrok-bridge on macOS CI, unified digital channel color schemes across the four GUIs, and the SLogic32U3’s four-channel maximum rate adjusted from 1600 to 1400 MHz (a specification correction), with ngscope defaulting to 40 MS/s sampling and 100 kS; scopehal-apps followed up by upgrading to the SLogic driver branch. V. Summary and Trend Observations

  • Security research enters a “reproducible” phase: UPC completed microarchitectural attack detection research using the commercial XuanTie C910 core and public datasets, advancing the RISC-V security narrative from “single-point vulnerability disclosure (GhostWrite, etc.)” to “quantitative detection plus dataset sharing”; for domestic cores, being selected as an experimental platform reflects both heightened attention and a reminder that commercial silicon needs proactive detection solutions.
  • Edge AI hardware and software reinforced in tandem: the Linux porting attempt for ESP32-S31, the new BM1688 single-board computer, SpacemiT llama.cpp v0.2.0, and Espressif esp-vision all point to edge inference moving from “it runs” to “it runs well” in the engineering phase; RISC-V continues to expand its form-factor boundaries from MCUs to edge boxes.
  • Dual-track rhythm in open-source communities: XiangShan is advancing vector, prediction, and cache microarchitectural tuning in parallel across multiple branches, maintaining a high development density; openRuyi this week focused on security updates and packaging specifications, awaiting the 2026.09 release window.
  • Embodied intelligence advancing in layers: after LingLong integrated the SONIC motion tracking policy, the robotics software stack continues to accumulate across three layers — “runtime interface, policy assets, simulation closed loop”; alongside academic activities such as the IISWC tutorial, the methodology building for RISC-V robotics workloads is unfolding in parallel on both the industry and academic sides.
  • Watch points: whether openRuyi 2026.09 will be released; the pace at which the official ESP32-S31 Linux BSP moves from developer preview to general availability; upstreaming developments for the SpacemiT ggml-spacemit backend.
