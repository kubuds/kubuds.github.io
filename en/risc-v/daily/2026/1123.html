Today’s Focus: RISC-V Club Established in Shanghai; China EV100 Says RISC-V Written into National Five-Year Plan for the First Time (11-19)

On November 19, the RISC-V Club founding ceremony and the “RISC-V Automotive Chip Technology and Application Ecosystem” closed-door seminar were held in Shanghai. The initiators were the China EV100 Research Institute and Infineon Technologies. More than 50 institutions participated, with over a hundred experts and corporate guests in attendance.

The attendee list covered major automakers. FAW, Dongfeng, Changan, Chery, Leapmotor, Li Auto, NIO, XPeng, Geely, SAIC, Volkswagen CARIAD, and Xiaomi Auto were all among them. On the industry chain side were CATARC, Tongji University, Valeo, United Automotive Electronic Systems, Luxshare Automotive, Horizon Robotics, iSOFT Infrastructure Software, Neusoft Reach, and AXERA. The event was positioned as a closed-door seminar, not a product launch.

The AURIX RC1 announced at the same event was already made public on November 4 and was covered in our November 20 issue. The new information in this issue is the Club as an organizational form and the positions formed at the seminar. The Club does not directly conduct chip R&D; its goal is to connect five segments: chips, toolchains, operating systems, vehicle validation, and standards development. Follow-up actions are joint research, technical seminars, and resource matching.

The policy language deserves separate note. An article by China EV100 states that the “15th Five-Year Plan for the Development of the Electronic Information Manufacturing Industry” has listed “advancing RISC-V R&D and industrialization” as a task item. The article says this is the first time RISC-V has been included in a national five-year plan. This statement currently appears only from China EV100; the original plan text has not been seen, so it is recorded as a single source.

The judgments formed at the seminar lean engineering-oriented. Mass production will first land in weak-ecosystem scenarios such as sensors, body control, TSN switching, and LiDAR, while cockpit remains at an early stage. Compute chips will advance in three tiers: “auxiliary cores, AI big-core management and control, and full-series RISC-V migration.” Attendees believe that in the AI era, the value of RISC-V lies not in peak compute but in modules such as “management and control plus safety island.”

The timelines given by automakers can be compared. Chery plans to gradually improve the automotive-grade ecosystem between 2027 and 2030 and achieve large-scale mass production. Li Auto has already deployed RISC-V small cores in its Mach chip and put them into mass production. CARIAD views RISC-V as a customizable unified architecture option but has not yet given a vehicle integration milestone.

Three caveats should be kept in mind. The Club is a collaborative platform and does not generate orders or SKUs. Automotive-grade AEC-Q, functional safety, and information security certifications still must be passed item by item. Open-source IP does not directly equal lower total cost; software construction and validation investment must be amortized through million-unit-scale mass production. In the short term, only two things can be observed: whether the Club publishes draft standards, and whether member companies announce specific vehicle models or domain controller projects.

I. Open Source Community and Software Ecosystem

XiangShan README Lists Tapeout Destinations for Each Generation for the First Time; 36Kr Long-Form Article Reports V100 Uses 6 XiangShan Cores (11-22)

On November 22, the main XiangShan repository merged #7021 into the kunminghu-v3 branch. The commit modifies the README, but the content is substantial: the original “Branch Maintenance Status” table was replaced with a “Version” table, adding a new “Tapeout” column.

The table names the tapeout destinations for each generation of cores for the first time. Yanqihu was taped out by MicroCore and Pengcheng Laboratory. Nanhu V2 is listed as Enterprise A and Enterprise B. Nanhu V3 was taped out by Innosilicon and Enterprise B. Kunminghu V1 is listed as Enterprise C. Kunminghu V2 was taped out by SpacemiT, with a note “more in progress.” The tapeout column for Kunminghu V3 is left blank.

The same table provides selection guidance. nanhu-v5 is for scenarios balancing performance and energy efficiency. kunminghu-v2 is for downstream research, customization, and high-performance, RVA23-compatible scenarios. kunminghu-v3 is recommended only for improving XiangShan itself, on the grounds that it is still iterating rapidly. The README’s update timestamp is marked as November 20, 2026.

On November 20, 36Kr published a long-form article titled “Understanding ‘XiangShan’: How an Open Source Processor Core Is Being Adopted by More Enterprises.” The figures cited provide a reference point. As of August 2026, the OpenXiangShan ecosystem has more than 100 partners. Two chips were taped out in 2025, with five expected in 2026. Of the 46 compute cores in SpacemiT’s V100, 6 come from XiangShan. Kunminghu V3 scores 22 SPECint2006 points per GHz.

The article also mentions product line expansion. Beyond CPU cores, XiangShan has introduced two categories of intelligent computing IP for high throughput and low latency, named Badaling and Xianglufeng. In May 2026, the first working group of the Open Source Chip Community—the Simultaneous Multithreading Working Group—was established, with ZTE Microelectronics and Alibaba DAMO Academy as founding members, agreeing to jointly develop on Kunminghu V3. On the repository side, there are over 7,400 GitHub stars and 985 branches.

Within the same window, kunminghu-v3 had four other code commits. A group merged on November 20 adds debug trigger support, including itrigger and etrigger, slot-zero icount in the Decode stage, and unified pipeline breakpoint and trigger actions. Another commit fixes a flushed CSR read, numbered #6853.

The significance of this information lies in “who is using it” moving from word of mouth to a table. The README is the file most easily cited by downstream in the repository; writing into the tapeout column amounts to providing a verifiable list. The anonymization of Enterprises A, B, and C also indicates that some customers still prefer not to be named. The tracking points are when Kunminghu V3 appears in the tapeout column, and whether the claim of five tapeouts in 2026 materializes.

RevyOS Kernel Enables Eight Platforms at Once, Including Zhihe A210 and ESWIN EIC7700 (11-22)

At 21:42 on November 22, the revyos/7.3.y branch of revyos/linux landed 20 commits at once. The timestamps are concentrated within the same minute, in the form of a batch push.

The configuration-layer changes enable eight platforms at once. Newly added are Zhihe A210, ESWIN EIC7700, UltraRISC DP1000, and SpacemiT K3 and K1. DAMO XuanTie TH1520, Sophgo SG2042 and SG2044 are also included, plus a Picoheart errata switch. ARM SCMI support is enabled at the same time.

These changes are in the same direction as moving the kernel into the Debian packaging layer on November 20: maintaining the kernel the way a distribution does, rather than modifying configuration per board.

Zhihe A210 is a newly added platform this issue. This SoC is eight-core at 1.9GHz, and software support previously concentrated mostly on vendor BSPs. RevyOS writing it into configuration means third-party distributions are beginning to maintain it as an official platform. On the same day, someone else got A210 running on NixOS; two independent threads point to the same conclusion: this board is entering third-party view.

RHEL 10.3 RISC-V Developer Preview Updated, Build Platform Migrated to SiFive BigSky 2U870 (11-16)

This is a backfill. Red Hat released the RHEL 10.3 RISC-V developer preview image on November 13, with a release notes article on November 16. This publication did not cover it in the issues from November 17 to 20; it is included now. The clue within the window is a discussion thread on Hacker News on November 20.

This is the fourth release of the preview. The image is based on RHEL 10.3, with Red Hat’s RISC-V team merging in as much upstream code as possible. The build platform has migrated from SiFive HiFive Premier P550 to the newly released SiFive BigSky 2U870. P550 remains compatible, but this is the last time it will be tested on it.

There are five categories of newly bootable platforms. SiFive BigSky 2U870 and SpacemiT V100 lead the list. SpacemiT K3’s Pico-ITX, CoM260, and RV2768 cluster server are also included. Also present are UltraRISC URDP1000 and Epic Semi Contrail AIX. The article states that most of these machines are 32 cores or more at 2GHz or higher, with V100 being dual-socket 2×46 cores.

The article also offers a comparison. In the early days with P550, compiling GCC or LLVM took days; now these 32-core platforms compress the time to hours. Red Hat also reminds that this is a developer preview, not production-supported, and hardware features are not guaranteed to be fully available.

The same article mentions that Fedora 45 for RISC-V has been released. Fedora is upstream of RHEL; the RISC-V version working first is a prerequisite for official RHEL support.

The judgment falls on the replacement of reference hardware. Red Hat switching its build and test baseline from a 4-core P550 to 32-core-class machines shows that its yardstick for evaluating RISC-V has changed. The bottleneck on the distribution side has shifted from “can it boot” to “is platform coverage sufficient.” What remains unanswered is when RHEL will move RISC-V from developer preview to official support.

Date: 2026-11-16　Source: Red Hat Developer: RHEL 10 Developer Preview for RISC-V updates to 10.3, RHEL 10 RISC-V developer preview product page, Hacker News discussion.

Spike Fixes Vector Tile Move Length; Shadow Stack Access Exception Reported as Store (11-21)

riscv-isa-sim (Spike) landed four commits between November 20 and 21. The core is two semantic corrections.

The first is vector tile move. The loop upper bound for the vtmv.t.v and vtmv.v.t instructions changed from P.VU.tn() to P.VU.tn(8 * sizeof(type)). The original form did not convert tile length by element width, so loop counts for 8-bit and 64-bit elements would be miscalculated. The change covers all four element widths of ZvT: 8, 16, 32, and 64.

The second is the exception type for the shadow stack. load_slow_path, when handling access exceptions, previously always raised a load access exception. Now it judges by the ss_access flag: when a shadow stack load hits an exception, it raises a store access exception. This corresponds to Zicfiss shadow stack semantics, where a stack read is an implicit store access in hardware.

Within the same window, the CORE-V CVW repository merged three PRs. These are Verilator lockstep comparison, a comparison configuration with the Sargantana core, and device tree changes for flash and SD. All three are verification and platform configuration, with no new instruction support.

Assessment: vector tile extensions like ZvT are becoming usable in Spike, a prerequisite for compiler and operator porting. The shadow stack exception type fix does not affect functionality but affects compliance testing and fault injection results. Both fall under the work of “the model must match the spec word for word.”

Date: 2026-11-21　Source: riscv-software-src/riscv-isa-sim, openhwfoundation/cvw.

II. Product and Ecosystem Updates

GUITION Launches 7-Inch ESP32-S31 Development Board, First to Use Native Ethernet (11-20)

On November 20, CNX Software reported that GUITION has listed the JC1060F570C smart display development board. The main controller is Espressif’s ESP32-S31, based on the RISC-V architecture.

The chip configuration is as follows. The high-performance core is RV32IMAFCP, clocked at 320MHz, with an FPU and SIMD. There is also a low-power MCU core. Memory consists of 512KB SRAM, 32KB low-power SRAM, and 16MB PSRAM, with 16MB flash. Wireless support includes 2.4GHz Wi-Fi 6, Bluetooth 5.4 (including LE Audio, direction finding, Mesh 1.1, and Classic Bluetooth), and 802.15.4.

The board-level configuration leans toward panels and gateways. It has a 7-inch IPS screen, 1024×600, with an RGB565 interface driven by the JD9165, plus capacitive touch. Audio uses the ES8389 codec plus an NS4150 amplifier, with two MEMS microphones. Interfaces include 10/100M Ethernet, one USB 2.0 high-speed and one full-speed port, two UARTs, I2C, and RS485.

Ethernet deserves separate mention. The ESP32-S31 itself supports gigabit, but GUITION only implemented 100M, presumably for cost reasons. This is the first development board to use this SoC’s native Ethernet interface. The two comparable products from Waveshare and VIEWE previously both had 4.3-inch screens and no native Ethernet.

Assessment: The ESP32-S31 puts a high-performance RISC-V core, Wi-Fi 6, 802.15.4, and native Ethernet on a single chip, targeting smart panels, gateways, and building control. Espressif’s RISC-V product line is moving from connectivity modules toward complete HMI solutions.

Date: 2026-11-20　Source: CNX Software: GUITION ESP32-S31 development kit features 7-inch capacitive touch display, Ethernet port.

RuyiSDK’s ruyi: Twenty Commits on Download Progress Events and porcelain Output (11-20)

On November 20, the ruyisdk/ruyi main repository landed 20 commits, focused on two things.

The first is download progress. A new progress event is reported at 1% or 1MiB steps, carrying a correlation ID so upper layers can map requests to progress. In porcelain mode, the human-readable progress bar is skipped in favor of structured event output.

The second is errors and robustness. Download failures are reported separately in porcelain mode, and troubleshooting hints are no longer printed. Illegal SemVer in manifest filenames no longer causes a crash. There is also an i18n refresh and ruff formatting.

Two PRs form the backbone of this batch. #520 handles robustness against malformed SemVer, and #521 introduces download progress and porcelain events. The remaining commits first abstract the logging and output interfaces at the fetcher layer, then fill in events.

Assessment: Changes like these don’t appear in the user-visible feature list, but they determine whether IDEs, CI, and graphical frontends can call ruyi as a component. RuyiSDK’s positioning is as the entry point for RISC-V development, and machine-readable output is essential infrastructure.

Date: 2026-11-20　Source: ruyisdk/ruyi.

III. Deep Dive on Domestic Teams

Zhihe A210: NixOS Runs on Eight Cores at 1.9GHz, with Sound Card, HDMI, Bluetooth, and Wi-Fi All Working (11-20)

On November 20, developer George Huebner published a blog post documenting the process of getting NixOS running on the Zhihe Computing A210. The A210 is a RISC-V SoC with eight cores at 1.9GHz.

The author’s use case was to turn this board into a NixOS build server. He cross-compiled from an x86_64 host to riscv64, building over 1,000 packages. Only two problems came up along the way: rust-bindgen used the wrong clang version, and ministream was not correctly configured for cross-compilation. Neither issue was related to RISC-V.

Hardware validation results were fairly complete. The sound card, HDMI, Bluetooth and Wi-Fi, Ethernet, and GPU all worked. The author also offered caveats: large packages like Clang and GFortran can consume over 9GB of memory in a single process during the linking stage, requiring parallelism to be reduced to 1 and runs to be left overnight.

Two methodological points are reusable. Nix’s pkgsCross.riscv64 needs only three lines of configuration to produce riscv64 executables. To expose multiple buildPlatforms simultaneously under flakes, the author used a wrapper configuration layer to mount the ISO and flashing scripts under packages.${system}.

Assessment: This is independent third-party validation of the A210, not a vendor BSP self-attestation. Eight cores at 1.9GHz is upper-mid-range among RISC-V application processors, and the ability to run desktop-class peripherals indicates improving platform maturity. The author’s own conclusion should be noted as well: he does not currently recommend buying any RISC-V SoC, because neither the ecosystem nor the price-performance ratio is there yet.

Date: 2026-11-20　Source: George Huebner: NixOS bring-up on an Alibaba RISC-V board, Hacker News discussion.

Sophgo sophon-tools Six Commits: Flashing Images Switch to Sector-Based Partition Tables, Device Identity Recheck Added Before Writing (11-22)

From November 21 to 22, sophgo/sophon-tools landed six commits focused on hardening the flashing toolchain.

Two commits at the partition layer. One changed the SE9 and SE13 flashing images from byte-based to sector-based partition table descriptions, version 1.5.0. The other preserves the local_update partition boundaries for SE9 and SE13 during writes, preventing accidental deletion during upgrades.

Three commits at the guard layer. The sgdisk pre-check in psocbak was moved earlier, sgdisk was bundled in-package, and a parsing issue with attribute values containing > was fixed, version 1.5.1. A test stub sgdisk pins the last partition boundary. psewriter was bumped to 2.2.0, adding a device identity recheck before writing plus an operation guard.

One cleanup commit. Fixed a non-portable gdisk path issue and removed build artifacts accidentally committed to the repo.

Assessment: These changes address real risks in mass production and after-sales support. Flashing the wrong device, writing the wrong partition, or using the wrong sgdisk version—any one of these can brick a board. Moving device identity recheck ahead of the write replaces “rescue after bricking” with “block before writing.” The switch to sector-based partition tables will affect compatibility with older firmware and tools, which will need to be upgraded accordingly.

Date: 2026-11-22　Source: sophgo/sophon-tools.

IV. Embodied Intelligence and Robotics Special Section

SpacemiT humanoid_common Pins OpenCV inpaint File Versions (11-20)

On November 20, spacemit-robotics/humanoid_common merged PR #39, branch name fix/hiking-depth-preprocessing. The change is a single item: the OpenCV inpaint data files used for depth hole-filling are now fetched at pinned versions.

This builds on the previous issue’s work. On November 19, #37 added configurable depth holes, noise, and latency to simulation, with the hole-filling algorithm using valid neighbors within a two-pixel radius. Hole-filling depends on OpenCV’s inpaint model files; if those files change with distribution or upstream updates, the same code produces different outputs on different machines.

The fix lists the required files and downloads them at pinned versions, rather than following the system OpenCV package wholesale. The commit title reads fetch only pinned OpenCV inpaint files, i.e., only the files on the list are fetched.

Assessment: The robotics stack is converging on “reproducibility.” Perception algorithms, simulation data, and model files all need version pinning; otherwise simulation and real hardware, or dev machines and vehicle-side systems, will produce mismatched results. Such commits are small, but they determine whether problems can be reproduced.

Date: 2026-11-20　Source: humanoid_common PR #39: fix Hiking depth preprocessing, spacemit-robotics/humanoid_common.

V. Summary and Trend Observations

The first observation is that XiangShan has written “who is taping out” into the repository. The tape-out column in the README names MicroCore, Pengcheng Laboratory, Innosilicon, and SpacemiT, with three other companies replaced by Enterprise A, B, and C. This is better suited to supply-chain verification than any launch event, and it also provides a basis for downstream selection. Note that Kunminghu V3 remains blank, indicating that the latest generation has no publicly disclosed tape-out partner yet.

The second observation is that distribution-side activity has shifted from “it runs” to “maintainable and integrable.” RevyOS opened eight platforms at once, turning platform support into configuration files. RHEL swapped its reference hardware from a 4-core P550 to a 32-core-class machine and is advancing Fedora 45 in parallel. ruyi is building machine-readable download events. What these three share is embedding RISC-V into existing engineering workflows rather than building a separate stack.

The third observation is that ecosystem organization precedes product landing. RISC-V Club brought together over 50 organizations at once, and policy language appeared in a national five-year plan for the first time. But automotive-grade certification, SKUs, and sample timelines are all still blank. Organization and policy can shorten the adoption period, but they cannot replace the validation cycle.

Three-Day Trend: Tape-Out List Goes Public, Platform Configurations Spread, Organization and Policy Fall into Place (11-20 ~ 11-22)

Looking at November 20 through 22 together, three threads advanced simultaneously. On the open-source core side, XiangShan used a README table to disclose the tape-out destinations of four generations of cores, while a 36Kr long-form article filled in ecosystem partners and shipment figures. On the foundational software side, RevyOS wrote eight platforms into kernel configurations, openRuyi completed Go packaging infrastructure, and ruyi added machine-readable output. On the industry side, RISC-V Club was established in Shanghai, bringing OEMs, Tier1s, and chip companies to the same table.

None of the three days’ items was a performance breakthrough. Verifiable progress centered on “writing clearly, configuring properly, and connecting up existing capabilities.” This is also the most common form RISC-V takes before entering mass production: lists, configuration files, and interfaces say more about where things stand than benchmarks do.
