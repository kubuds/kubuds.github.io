Today’s Focus: StarFive JH-B100 Reaches Mass Production and Delivery, RISC-V Server BMC Enters Customer Systems (11-17)

On November 17, StarFive announced that its RISC-V-based server BMC (Baseboard Management Controller) chip, the JH-B100 (昉·惊鸿-B100), has achieved mass production and been delivered to customers. The official statement is that the chip has passed product introduction and review processes at multiple server manufacturers and internet companies, and customer server products carrying it are about to enter mass production. Shipment volumes, customer names, and production schedules were not disclosed.

Specifications per StarFive’s official product page: a quad-core in-house RISC-V core (19,200 DMIPS) with a coherent network-on-chip (NoC), compatible with DDR4-3200 and DDR5-4800, integrating PFR (Platform Firmware Resilience) and PROT functions, supporting Chinese cryptographic standards SM2/SM3/SM4, secure boot, and DICE; interfaces include dual PCIe 4.0 endpoints, full-featured eSPI Slave, PECI, I2C/I3C, USB 3.2 Gen1, SGPIO, CAN-FD, IPMB, MCTP, and NCSI, with two built-in LTPI controllers to support dual-node management; the accompanying SDK follows the Yocto standard and can connect directly to OpenBMC. Beyond CPU server motherboards, applications also include compute trays, switch trays, coolant distribution units, and power shelves in AI infrastructure.

The significance of this news lies in the “position” rather than “performance.” The BMC does not participate in computing, but every server needs one, and this position has long been held by Arm and 8051-class controllers; previously, RISC-V had only sampling and validation at this position, with no mass production delivery. The logic for choosing it as a breakthrough point is also clear: performance requirements are far lower than the main CPU, the software stack (OpenBMC, Yocto) is mature, and the validation cost for customer introduction is manageable. The ecosystem endorsements StarFive cites are Intel and AMI—the former completed validation of Intel CPU management functions on the CRB platform, and the latter integrated the JH-B100 SDK into its BMC firmware product OneTree; both were disclosed previously at Intel Connection and the Dishui Lake Forum, and this issue marks the first time they have reached the “mass production delivery” stage.

Viewed with restraint, several points still warrant observation. First, “about to enter mass production” is the customer-side framing; actual volume depends on system manufacturers’ production schedules, and BMC procurement decisions often follow the main CPU platform. Second, StarFive did not provide shipment volumes, making it impossible to judge whether this is small-batch delivery or volume shipment. Third, the long-term test for RISC-V BMC lies in firmware maintenance and security updates; OpenBMC’s community support and CVE response speed will need to run through one or two product cycles before they can be assessed. On the same day, the MIIT-administered media and several industry media outlets republished this news, narrating it alongside the “15th Five-Year Plan” item on “advancing R&D and industrialization of the fifth-generation reduced instruction set,” a clear indication that the policy context and commercial progress are being tied together.

I. Open-Source Community and Software Ecosystem

openRuyi’s Nineteen Commits: OpenSBI Bumped to 1.9, OpenSSL 3.6.5 Introduced (11-17)

The openRuyi main repository landed 19 package commits over the course of November 17. On the firmware side, OpenSBI was updated to 1.9; the crypto stack gained openssl-3 at version 3.6.5, with CI builds switched to prefer OpenSSL; base libraries bumped krb5 to 1.22.3 and fixed dnf5 builds under json-c 0.19; on the desktop side, openruyi-desktop-setup-kde received missing dependency declarations. The official news page’s latest items remain the two from October 19 (ecosystem infrastructure, Ruyi Firmware) and the 2026.09 release; no new version appeared within the window.

revyos/linux Overhauls 7.3.y Config Baseline, Lands 20 Platform Patches at Once (11-18)

At 04:13 on November 18, revyos/linux pushed 20 config commits to the revyos/7.3.y branch in a single batch—all 20 share an identical commit timestamp, with author dates reaching back to December 2025, indicating a bulk landing after a branch overhaul rather than work done overnight. The content enables options platform by platform: ESWIN EIC7700, UltraRISC DP1000, SpacemiT K3 and K1, T-Head TH1520, Sophgo SG2042/SG2044, plus a Picoheart errata switch and a set of generic options; it also adds cross/native build CI, a linuxboot config and debian_defconfig, and changes the package naming rule to use the major version number.

Aligning config files across six platforms at once shows that RevyOS has shifted from “a distro for one board” to “one kernel config covering multiple RISC-V product lines.” For downstream vendors, the value of such changes is less config work: when a new board arrives, first check whether the defconfig has a switch for your SoC—far faster than writing a dts from scratch. The risk lies in the same place—one branch simultaneously carries errata and hacks for six platforms (commits include workarounds forcing the xe driver onto DG1/DG2), so any one vendor’s change can affect another’s regression; adding cross and native build CI provides a safeguard.

Two CORE-V Items: RVFI CSR Logging Merged, CVW Reverts Dependency Cleanup (11-17)

At 06:20 on November 17, openhwfoundation/core-v-verif merged PR #2675 (rvfi-csr-logging from ankit-cybertron), adding CSR logging to the RVFI interface. The same day, CVW first merged PR #2099 (clearing uv.lock dependency warnings and enabling uv updates), then reverted it entirely with a follow-up commit.

RVFI is the observation port for RISC-V formal verification and differential testing: it exposes each instruction’s PC, register reads/writes and memory accesses to the verifier, but CSR state was previously absent, so privileged-mode behavior could only be compared indirectly. With CSR logging, per-instruction changes to registers like mstatus and mtvec can be checked directly, which is especially useful for verifying exception and interrupt paths. The CVW revert is a different kind of signal—dependency-warning cleanup looks harmless, but once lock files and update policies change, regression-environment reproducibility suffers, so a revert is preferable to proceeding with uncertainty.

Radxa Linkr releases v1.4.2 through v1.6.0 in a batch, with plugin center and H.265 encoding in place (11-17)

Radxa’s browser-based remote management product line Linkr released four versions in a batch at around 15:2x on November 17: v1.4.2, v1.5.0, v1.5.2, and v1.6.0, with all four releases published less than five minutes apart; on the same day, the harness-linkr repository was also newly created with an Initial commit. Four versions appearing on the same day looks more like historical versions being backfilled all at once after the repository went public, and should not be read as four releases in a single day.

According to the release notes, v1.6.0 adds H.265 encoding, custom HTTPS certificates, bidirectional clipboard sync (requiring installation of the executable bundled with the Linkrd plugin on the controlled machine), a 720p 180fps EDID preset, and a watermark toggle; v1.5.0 introduces a plugin center (upload, install, start/stop, uninstall), two networking plugins for ZeroTier and EasyTier, screenshot OCR, experimental IPv6, and adds toggle control to the Agent API; v1.5.2 supports double-tapping the FN key to toggle the Wi-Fi hotspot, automatically selecting 5GHz or 2.4GHz based on the connected band, and fixes the inability to switch resolution when a Radxa Dragon Q8B is used as a video input source; v1.4.2 adds audio to H.264 WebSocket mode, disables Wi-Fi by default to reduce power consumption, and adds experimental touch input.

Taken together, Linkr’s direction is “orchestratable out-of-band management”: the plugin center handles feature extension, the Agent API handles external invocation, the networking plugins handle cross-subnet access, and OCR and clipboard handle human-in-the-loop operations. III. Deep Dive into Domestic Teams

SpacemiT xslim: Quantization Stack Pushes Five Commits in a Row on agent Branch (11-17)

SpacemiT’s model compression toolchain xslim pushed five commits in a row on November 17 on the agent/feat/operator-layer-quantization branch (from 08:46 to 19:35), all centered on quantization strategy: selecting per-layer calibration methods based on user data, parsing and validating a unified precision plan, validating fusion provenance and effective precision boundaries, budgeted automatic mixed precision, and task-metric search with controlled local fp16. Another branch, agent/chore/add-agent-harness, added a repo-level agent harness and contribution guide on November 13.

The direction of these commits is “turning quantization from hand-written configuration into search”: given a precision budget and a task metric, let the tool decide which layers use which precision, rather than manually filling in a table for each layer. At the same time, provenance and fused validation appear repeatedly, indicating they have been burned by fused-operator precision mismatches on real models—a quantized convolution gets fused by the backend, and the precision actually in effect is not the same as what the config file says; without validation, you get a silent discrepancy where “the config says int8 but it’s running fp16.” The naming of the agent branch is also worth noting: the toolchain is starting to organize code and skills around “for use by automated agents,” rather than only for humans.

On the same day, spacemit-com/onnxruntime merged an upstream change (selecting model package variants by EP priority); this is upstream synchronization and does not involve RISC-V-specific changes. The xslim main branch had no commits during the window; all progress is on feature branches.

Date: 2026-11-17　Source: spacemit-com/xslim (agent/feat/operator-layer-quantization), spacemit-com/onnxruntime

Sophgo sophon-tools: se-rag-core Adds Layered Model Configuration and Bounded Fallback (11-17)

Sophgo’s sophon-tools submitted a feat(se-rag-core) commit on November 17 at 13:30: layered OpenAI configuration, plus CF-first with bounded fallback for the built-in gateway (commit IDs MYS-2924 / MYS-292x). The changes are concentrated in the RAG component’s model access layer—splitting the OpenAI-compatible configuration into multiple layers (default, environment, deployment level) and merging them by priority; the gateway portion preferentially uses the CF (Cloudflare) route and falls back a limited number of times after failure.

This is not technically sophisticated, but the direction is clear: the LLM application stack on the SE series (SG2042 / SG2044 servers) is moving toward operability. Layered configuration solves the problem of “the same codebase using different model endpoints in development, testing, and at customer sites”; CF-first with bounded fallback solves cross-border access stability, and the number of fallbacks must be bounded—otherwise a single failure will send requests to all backup routes. It should be noted that this is an application-layer tool, not a chip or driver; this report includes it because it reflects that what Sophgo is supplementing beyond server hardware is the “out-of-the-box usable LLM service” segment.

IV. Special Chapter on Embodied Intelligence and Robotics

SpacemiT Robotics Stack Integrates Hiking Perceptive Locomotion Policy, Depth Maps Enter Policy Loop via ZMQ (11-17)

At 16:04–16:05 on November 17, two repositories under spacemit-robotics each merged one commit: PR #33 “support Hiking perceptive locomotion” in humanoid_common and PR #20 “add Hiking perceptive control simulation” in humanoid_unitree_g1, both authored by chenzhaoqi. The humanoid_common change spans 16 files with 938 lines added, covering: a Hiking policy adaptation layer (depth encoding, observation history, joint mapping, model checksum), publishing MuJoCo camera depth via ZMQ using offscreen EGL rendering, validating depth metadata, timestamps, and history length before entering RL, support for online RL handover (action history initialization, readiness checks, preparation feedback), retaining the current policy on handover failure or timeout, and adding perceptive build dependencies with rebuild of consumers when RL headers change.

Judging from the commit scope, this interface corresponds to the publicly available Hiking perceptive locomotion policy (Hiking in the Wild, Tsinghua IIIS and Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute, Unitree G1 with a front-facing depth camera, depth at 60Hz, policy at 50Hz); the official source has not stated the exact origin of the adaptation target, so this is an inference based on the commit contents. Three engineering points worth noting: retaining the old policy on handover failure is a safety fallback, preventing the robot from losing control at the moment of policy switching; the model checksum prevents policy files from being replaced or version-mismatched; and rebuild-on-header-change exists because they hit the C++ struct layout mismatch pitfall—the commit notes state that staged installs preserve source file timestamps, so when RL headers change but cached objects are not recompiled, the linked struct layouts do not match.

Making third-party policies into a replaceable adaptation layer is a necessary step for a robotics stack moving toward a “policy ecosystem”: a chip vendor cannot train all policies itself; what it can do is standardize the three segments of perception input, policy inference, and action handover, so that externally trained policies can run as soon as they are plugged in. Platforms like K3 provide vector and multi-core capabilities at this position, along with headroom for running MuJoCo and depth rendering.

manipulation and manifest Wrap-Up: motion-completion Docs Added, Simulated Camera Output Added to Ignore List (11-17)

On the same day, the manipulation and manifest repositories under spacemit-robotics landed six commits in total. V. Summary and Trend Observations

Today there is only one main thread: the management plane lands first. The mass-production delivery of StarFive JH-B100 marks the first time RISC-V has secured a “mass-production delivery” position in the data center rather than “sample validation.” The BMC does not participate in computing, but every server needs one, and volume scales with the whole machine; its performance requirements are far lower than the main CPU, the software stack is backed by OpenBMC and Yocto, customer onboarding validation costs are manageable, and the supply chain has long offered only two choices: Arm and 8051. Breaking through at this position is far more realistic than a head-on collision on the server main CPU.

The second judgment is that the software side today is “catching up on versions” rather than “shipping new things.” openRuyi pushes OpenSBI to 1.9, introduces OpenSSL 3.6.5, and brings xgrammar and tvm-ffi into the repository; revyos reorganizes the kernel configuration baseline, arranging the switches for six platforms in one pass. Neither will make headlines, but they determine whether customers can avoid tweaking configurations and directly use existing inference stacks after receiving a board. A large part of RISC-V’s software gap hides in this kind of daily alignment.

The third judgment is that progress on the robotics side lands in the “adaptation layer.” SpacemiT plugs the Hiking perceptive policy into humanoid_common, using ZMQ to transmit depth maps, checksums to validate models, and retaining the old policy on handover failure—these are engineering details, not paper metrics, but they determine whether third-party policies can run on K3. A chip vendor cannot do all policies; what it can do is standardize the interface, and today there was concrete action on this.

Three-Day Trend: Management Plane Lands First, Software Stack Catches Up on Versions, Robotics Stack Adds Perception (11-15 ~ 11-17)

Looking at November 15 through 17 together, three lines each advanced. On the whole-machine and chip side, Purism launched the Librem 5 v2 crowdfunding on November 14 and issued an official press release on November 17 refining the delivery timeline to “14 to 15 months after the crowdfunding ends”; StarFive pushed the RISC-V BMC to mass-production delivery on November 17. Both add weight to “RISC-V entering the whole-machine BOM”—one is a consumer phone, the other a data center server management chip.

On the open-source core and verification side, FURSOY on November 15 unveiled an out-of-order RV64 core capable of booting Linux 6.6.50; on November 16 XiangShan was closing out memory access queue defects while CVW was filling in vector memory access; on November 17 core-v-verif brought CSR state into the RVFI log while XiangShan did naming refactoring. What these four share is a shift from “it runs” to “it is verifiable and maintainable,” which is also the precondition for open-source cores to enter product view.

On the software and toolchain side, openRuyi spent two consecutive days on version tracking and configuration alignment, while SpacemiT worked on automated search in the quantization toolchain and policy adaptation in the robotics stack. Taken together, the window for architectural narrative is narrowing and the window for engineering delivery is opening: over the next six months, what determines RISC-V’s position will not be another new chip launch event, but whether this kind of delivery, configuration, and adaptation can be sustained.
